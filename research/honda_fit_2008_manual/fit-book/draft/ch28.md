## The full explanation

### Your battery, and why you check it monthly

A battery stores electrical power and delivers a big burst of it to start your engine. Your Fit's owner's manual tells you to check the battery's condition once a month.<!-- F-WP09-061 --> Most batteries have a small test indicator window on top. A label on the battery explains what each color in that window means.<!-- F-WP09-061 --> The window's exact location can vary between battery brands, so check the label on your own battery.<!-- F-WP09-061 -->

The battery that came with your Fit is a group size 151R.<!-- F-WP09-046 --> A "group size" is a standard code for a battery's physical dimensions and terminal layout, so the right one fits your battery tray and connects correctly. Some owners report swapping in a group 51R battery instead, since it offers more cold-cranking amps, a rating of how much starting power a battery can deliver in cold weather. Owners report that a 51R is a noticeably tighter physical fit, and some say they had to modify the battery tray to make it work.<!-- F-WP09-046 --> For a straightforward replacement with no modification, stick with the original 151R size.<!-- F-WP09-046 -->

A battery does one thing you should always respect: it gives off hydrogen gas, an explosive gas, during normal operation. This is true every time you work near the battery, for any reason. Keep flames, sparks, and cigarettes away from it, and always wear eye protection when you work close to it.

### Testing the battery with a multimeter

A multimeter is a small handheld tool that reads electrical voltage, among other things. To test your battery, you touch its two probes to the battery's two posts and read the number on the screen. A rested battery, meaning the engine has been off at least an hour, should read about 12.6 volts or higher if it is fully charged.<!-- P-WP11-10 --> A reading near 12.2 volts means the battery is only about half charged, and anything below 12.0 volts usually points to a weak or failing battery.<!-- P-WP11-10 --> You can also have a helper crank the engine while you watch the meter; a healthy battery should not drop below about 9.6 volts while doing that.<!-- P-WP11-10 --> Full steps are below.

### Cleaning corroded terminals

Corrosion looks like a white or yellowish, crusty powder around the battery posts and cable clamps. It forms slowly over time as the battery works, and it can weaken your electrical connection or even stop the car from starting. Baking soda mixed with water reacts with this corrosion and breaks it apart, letting you rinse it away. A thin coat of grease afterward slows corrosion from coming right back.

### Replacing the battery

Before you disconnect any cable, write down your radio's 5-digit anti-theft code. Many Honda audio systems lock themselves as soon as the battery loses power, and you need that code to unlock the radio again afterward. Always disconnect the negative cable first and reconnect it last; this order lowers the risk of a spark shorting something in the car's electrical system. The new battery needs to be the same group size, 151R, so it fits the tray and hold-down bracket, the metal piece that clamps the battery in place, without modification.<!-- F-WP09-046 -->

## Step by step

### Test the battery with a multimeter

**Safety warnings, before you touch a probe to the battery:**
- Always wear eye protection when working near the battery. It gives off explosive hydrogen gas during normal operation.
- Do not smoke or bring sparks or flames near the battery while testing it.
- Do not let the multimeter's metal probes touch both terminals, or any metal, at the same time by accident. This can cause a spark.
- Always wash your hands after handling the battery or its terminals.

**Steps:**
1. Turn off the engine and every accessory, such as lights, radio, and fan, and let the car sit at least 1 hour.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
2. Set the multimeter dial to 20 volts DC.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
3. Touch the red probe to the positive (+) battery post.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
4. Touch the black probe to the negative (-) battery post.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
5. Read the number on the multimeter's screen. This is the resting voltage.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
6. Compare the reading to this chart: 12.6 volts or higher means a full, healthy charge.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
7. A reading near 12.2 volts means the battery is only about half charged.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
8. A reading below 12.0 volts usually means a weak or failing battery.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
9. Optional: have a helper crank the engine while you watch the meter. A healthy battery should not drop below about 9.6 volts while cranking.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->
10. If the reading is low, or the battery struggles to crank the engine, plan to charge or replace it.<!-- P-WP11-10 -->

**How you know it worked:** you have a clear resting-voltage number and, if you did the cranking check, a cranking-voltage number, and you know which category the battery falls into.

### Replace the battery

**Safety warnings, before you loosen a cable clamp:**
- Always wear eye protection and protective clothing, or have a skilled technician do this. The battery gives off explosive hydrogen gas.
- Do not smoke or bring sparks or flames near the battery.
- Always disconnect the negative (-) cable first, and reconnect it last, to reduce the risk of a spark shorting the vehicle's electrical system.
- Always wash your hands after handling the battery, its posts, or its terminals. They contain lead and lead compounds.
- Do not drop the battery. It is heavy and can crack, spilling acid.

**Steps:**
1. Turn off the engine and every accessory.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
2. Write down or confirm you have your radio's 5-digit anti-theft code.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
3. Loosen and remove the negative (-) cable clamp first, and set it aside so it cannot touch the terminal.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
4. Loosen and remove the positive (+) cable clamp next.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
5. Remove the bolt or clamp holding the battery hold-down bracket in place.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
6. Lift the old battery straight out, using a strap or careful grip. It is heavy.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
7. Set the new battery in the tray, in the same orientation as the old one.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
8. Reinstall the hold-down bracket and its bolt.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
9. Reconnect the positive (+) cable clamp first this time, and tighten it.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
10. Reconnect the negative (-) cable clamp last, and tighten it.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
11. Start the engine to confirm it runs normally.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
12. Turn on the radio and enter your 5-digit code if "ENTER CODE" appears.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->
13. Reset the clock if it lost its setting.<!-- P-WP11-11 -->

**How you know it worked:** the engine starts normally, the radio accepts your code, or never asked for one, and the clock is reset if needed.

### Clean the battery terminals

**Safety warnings, before you mix any solution:**
- Always wear eye protection and protective clothing, or have a skilled technician do this. The battery gives off explosive hydrogen gas.
- Do not smoke or bring sparks or flames near the battery.
- Always wash your hands after handling the battery or its terminals. They contain lead and lead compounds.
- Always disconnect the negative (-) cable first if you need to remove a cable, and reconnect it last.

**Steps:**
1. Look at each terminal for a white or yellowish powder. This is corrosion.<!-- P-WP11-12 -->
2. Mix baking soda with a little water into a thick paste.<!-- P-WP11-12 -->
3. Cover the corroded terminal with the baking soda and water solution.<!-- P-WP11-12 -->
4. Watch it bubble up and turn brown as it reacts with the corrosion.<!-- P-WP11-12 -->
5. Once the bubbling stops, wash the terminal off with plain water.<!-- P-WP11-12 -->
6. Dry the battery and terminals with a cloth or paper towel.<!-- P-WP11-12 -->
7. Coat the clean terminals with a thin layer of grease to help prevent future corrosion.<!-- P-WP11-12 -->
8. Check that your radio still works. If it shows "ENTER CODE," enter your 5-digit code.<!-- P-WP11-12 -->

**How you know it worked:** the white or yellow powder is gone, the terminals look clean and shiny, and the cable clamps sit tight on the posts with no wobble.

## What can go wrong

- **Testing right after driving, before the battery has rested.** This gives a falsely high reading. Wait at least an hour with the engine off.
- **Assuming a battery that reads 12.6 volts resting is automatically fine.**<!-- P-WP11-10 --> A battery can pass a resting-voltage test and still fail under starter load. The cranking check catches that.
- **Disconnecting the positive cable first.** This raises the risk of a short if a tool accidentally touches metal. Always take the negative cable off first.
- **Forgetting the radio code before disconnecting the battery.** You may have to dig through old paperwork afterward to find it.
- **Not securing the hold-down bracket.** A loose battery can shift while driving.
- **Buying a non-original battery size without checking the physical fit first.** A group 51R battery is a tighter squeeze than the factory 151R.<!-- F-WP09-046 -->
- **Skipping the grease coating after cleaning terminals.** Corrosion often comes right back within months.
- **Not washing off all the baking soda paste.** This leaves a residue behind on the terminal.
