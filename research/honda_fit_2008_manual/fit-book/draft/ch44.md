# Chapter 44: Exhaust and O2 sensors

## What you will learn

- What an oxygen sensor does, and why your Fit has two of them
- How to replace an upstream or downstream oxygen sensor
- How to replace a rusted or broken section of exhaust pipe or muffler
- The warning signs that a job is too big for your driveway
- What to check before you call either repair finished

## The short version

Your Fit's exhaust system carries hot gas away from the engine. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> Two small sensors along the way check the oxygen level in that gas. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> You can replace either sensor, or a rusted section of pipe or muffler, using basic tools. <!-- P-WP14-12, P-WP14-13 --> Always let the exhaust cool first, since these parts stay dangerously hot long after you shut off the engine. <!-- P-WP14-12, P-WP14-13 -->

## The full explanation

### What an oxygen sensor does

An oxygen sensor is a small part that threads into your exhaust pipe. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> It measures how much oxygen is left in the exhaust gas. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> Your Fit's engine has one exhaust bank, and two of these sensors sit along it. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->

The upstream sensor sits closer to the engine. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> It is also called the primary sensor, or the air-fuel-ratio sensor. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> It threads into the exhaust manifold, the metal pipe that collects gas straight from the engine's cylinders. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> The downstream sensor sits farther back, on the catalytic converter. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> A catalytic converter is a part that cleans pollutants out of the exhaust gas. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> The downstream sensor watches how well that converter is working. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->

These two sensors connect to check-engine codes covered in Chapter 35. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> Code P0135 flags a problem with the upstream sensor's heater. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> Code P0141 flags the same kind of problem on the downstream sensor. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> A third code, P0420, means the computer thinks your catalytic converter is not cleaning the exhaust well enough. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> That code can also show up because of a bad upstream sensor or an engine misfire. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> Always rule those two causes out before you blame the converter itself. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->

Reaching the downstream sensor's electrical connector is harder than reaching the upstream one. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> On this car, you likely need to remove the center console from inside the car first. <!-- P-WP14-12 --> That is why this book rates the upstream sensor job easier than the downstream one, even though the two sensors look similar. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->

### Exhaust pipes and mufflers

Your exhaust system is a chain of pipe sections and a muffler. <!-- P-WP14-13 --> These parts join at flanges, which are flat joints held together with bolts. <!-- P-WP14-13 --> Rubber hangers hold the whole system up under the car. <!-- P-WP14-13 --> Rust and road salt attack these flanges and hangers over the years. <!-- P-WP14-13 --> Eventually a section rusts through, or a joint seizes shut. <!-- P-WP14-13 --> This book could not find a Fit-specific, bolt-by-bolt guide for this exact car's exhaust system. <!-- P-WP14-13 --> The steps below use standard technique for this type of car and system. <!-- P-WP14-13 --> They are not a Honda-confirmed script written for the 2008 Fit specifically. <!-- P-WP14-13 -->

## Step by step

### Replacing an oxygen (O2) sensor

What you need: <!-- P-WP14-12 -->
- An oxygen-sensor socket (a socket with a slot cut for the sensor's wire), or a wrench that fits
- Penetrating oil, for a rusted sensor
- Anti-seize compound made for oxygen sensors — a paste that keeps metal parts from rusting together
- Jack and 2 jack stands, if you work from underneath
- Basic interior trim tools, only if you replace the downstream sensor and need to remove the center console
- A new sensor made for the exact position you are replacing. Upstream and downstream sensors are different parts. This book could not confirm a part number this pass. Ask a Honda parts counter, or check Appendix F.

Safety warnings: <!-- P-WP14-12 -->
- Always let the exhaust system cool completely before you touch it. It stays dangerously hot long after the engine shuts off.
- Always wear eye protection, especially when you use penetrating oil on a rusted sensor.
- Do not work under a car held up only by a jack. Use jack stands.
- Do not overtighten a new sensor. Overtightening can crack the sensor body.

Steps: <!-- P-WP14-12 -->
1. Make sure the exhaust system is completely cool before you start.
2. Decide whether you are replacing the upstream sensor or the downstream sensor.
3. Raise the car and support it on jack stands, if you need to work from underneath.
4. Disconnect the sensor's electrical connector. For the downstream sensor, you may need to remove the center console first.
5. Spray penetrating oil on the sensor's threads if it looks rusted. Let it sit a few minutes.
6. Use your oxygen-sensor socket or wrench to loosen and remove the old sensor.
7. Compare the new sensor's connector and threads to the old one, to confirm they match.
8. Thread the new sensor in by hand first, so you do not cross the threads.
9. Tighten it with the socket or wrench until snug. Do not overtighten it.
10. Reconnect the sensor's electrical connector.
11. Reinstall the center console, if you removed it for the downstream sensor.
12. Lower the car, if you raised it.
13. If you have a scan tool, clear any stored check-engine codes tied to the sensor. Then take a test drive.

How you know it worked: The check-engine light stays off for the code you were chasing, after a normal test drive. If you have a scan tool, the sensor's reading changes as the engine runs, instead of staying flat. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->

### Replacing an exhaust section or muffler

What you need: <!-- P-WP14-13 -->
- Jack and 2 jack stands
- Penetrating oil
- A wrench and socket set for the flange bolts or clamps
- A cutting tool, such as a rotary tool or a hacksaw, for a seized joint
- A rubber mallet, to help free a rusted flange
- New gaskets (thin seals that stop exhaust leaks) and new self-locking nuts (nuts with a built-in insert that resists loosening on its own) for every flange joint you open. Honda's own guidance for this car calls for fresh ones, not reused ones.
- New rubber hangers, for any that are cracked or torn
- The replacement pipe, muffler, or section itself. This book could not confirm a part number this pass. Check with a Honda parts counter, a muffler shop, or Appendix F.

Safety warnings: <!-- P-WP14-13 -->
- Always let the exhaust system cool completely before you touch it.
- Always wear eye protection, especially when you use penetrating oil or a cutting tool on rusted fasteners.
- Do not work under a car held up only by a jack. Use jack stands.
- Do not cut near the fuel tank, fuel lines, or brake lines. Know what sits above and around a spot before you cut it.
- Always support a heavy section, like the muffler, before you loosen its last hanger. It should not swing down and hit you or the ground.

Steps: <!-- P-WP14-13 -->
1. Make sure the exhaust system is completely cool before you start.
2. Raise the car and support it on jack stands.
3. Spray penetrating oil on every fastener and hanger rubber you plan to disturb. Let it sit.
4. Support the section you are removing with a jack, a strap, or a helper, before you loosen its last fastener.
5. Separate the flange joint at each end of the section. Use a rubber mallet if it is rusted tight.
6. If a clamp or bolt is fully seized, cut it carefully rather than force it and damage the pipe around it.
7. Remove the old section, working it free of its rubber hangers.
8. Compare the new section to the old one, to confirm it matches, before you install it.
9. Fit new rubber hangers if the old ones are cracked or torn.
10. Position the new section and hang it loosely on its rubber mounts before you tighten anything.
11. Fit new gaskets at each flange joint you opened.
12. Install new self-locking nuts at those flange joints.
13. Tighten all the flange fasteners, with the system hanging naturally, not twisted or under strain.
14. Lower the car and start the engine. Listen and look for any exhaust leak or rattle.

How you know it worked: There is no hissing, ticking, or rattling from the exhaust while the engine idles. There is no visible leak at any joint. The exhaust hangs evenly, without touching the body or the ground. <!-- P-WP14-13 -->

## What can go wrong

### Oxygen sensor mistakes

- Confusing the upstream and downstream sensor, and ordering the wrong part. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->
- Overtightening the new sensor and cracking its body. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->
- Forcing a rusted sensor without penetrating oil, and snapping it off inside the exhaust manifold or converter. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->
- Not clearing the old fault code. The check-engine light can stay on even after a correct repair, until enough clean drive cycles pass. <!-- P-WP14-12 -->

### Exhaust and muffler mistakes

- Reusing old gaskets or nuts at a flange joint instead of new ones, which can cause a leak later. <!-- P-WP14-13 -->
- Fully tightening one flange before the whole section is loosely hung, which can twist and stress the joints. <!-- P-WP14-13 -->
- Cutting a stuck fastener without checking what sits directly behind or above it first. <!-- P-WP14-13 -->
- Not supporting a heavy section before you remove its last hanger. <!-- P-WP14-13 -->
