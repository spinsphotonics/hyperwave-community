# Chapter 34: How to Describe a Problem

## What you will learn
- Why a clear description helps you find the real cause of a car problem
- How to describe *when* a noise, vibration, smell, or leak happens
- How to describe *where* you feel or notice it
- How your speed and engine speed give useful clues
- How color and smell narrow down a leak or an odor
- The difference between a "Fit-specific" finding and general automotive knowledge, a difference you will see again in Chapter 37

## The short version
A good description has four parts: when it happens, where you notice it, how fast you were going, and what it looks or smells like. Chapters 35 through 37 sort information this same way, so a clear description lets you use those chapters fast. Write your notes down before you call a shop or look up a code.

## The full explanation

### Why this matters

"My car is making a weird noise" tells a mechanic almost nothing. It also will not help you use the tables in Chapter 37 or the flowchart in Chapter 36. The more specific you are, the faster anyone, including you, can narrow down the cause.

This chapter teaches you to notice four things about any problem: when it happens, where you notice it, your speed, and what it looks or smells like. These are the same four categories this book's research used to build the lookup tables in Chapter 37.<!-- WP-12 Sections C, D, E, F: table structure -->

### When does it happen?

Ask yourself when the noise, shake, or smell shows up. Common answers include: at idle, meaning while the engine runs but the car is not moving; while accelerating; while turning; while braking; or over bumps.<!-- WP-12 Section C: "When" column categories -->

For example, a ticking sound at idle often points to a low oil level or a problem in the VTEC system, the mechanism that switches the intake valves between a low-lift and a high-lift setting using engine oil pressure.<!-- F-WP12-010 --><!-- F-WP12-011 --> A clunk that happens only when you shift into Drive or Reverse points to something different: a worn engine or transmission mount, the rubber-and-metal part that holds the engine or transmission to the car's frame.<!-- F-WP02-016 --> Two very different sounds, both "at idle," but the timing detail (constant tick vs. one-time clunk on shifting) points to two different systems.

### Where do you notice it?

Location matters too. Do you feel a vibration through the steering wheel, or through the seat and floor? Do you hear a noise from the front of the car, the middle, or the back? Owners report that a clicking noise that only happens while turning often points to a worn CV joint, short for constant velocity joint, a flexible joint in the axle that lets power reach the wheel while it turns.<!-- WP-12 Section C: CV joint clicking while turning, community-reported --> That same clicking sound, if it does not change while you turn the wheel, more likely points to a loose exhaust heat shield, a metal panel that protects nearby parts from a hot exhaust pipe.<!-- WP-12 Section C: heat shield rattle, community-reported -->

### How fast were you going?

Speed is one of the most useful clues for a vibration. A shimmy in the steering wheel that appears below about 20 mph and goes away above that speed often points to a tire that is out of round, a bent wheel, or a stuck brake part dragging.<!-- WP-12 Section D: low-speed shimmy, general automotive knowledge --> A stronger shake that grows worse between roughly 45 and 65 mph often points to a wheel or tire that is out of balance, meaning its weight is not spread evenly around the wheel.<!-- WP-12 Section D: mid-to-highway speed shake, community-reported plus general knowledge --> Writing down the exact speed range helps you or a mechanic skip straight to the likely cause.

Engine speed matters too, separately from road speed. A whine or grind from the front of the engine that gets louder as the engine revs, not as the car speeds up, points toward the alternator, the part that charges the battery while the engine runs, or the belt that spins it.<!-- WP-12 Section C: alternator noise, community-reported -->

### What does it look or smell like?

For a leak, note the color and exactly where under the car you see it. Amber or dark brown usually means engine oil.<!-- WP-12 Section F: engine oil leak, general automotive knowledge --> Red or pink usually means automatic transmission fluid on this car, since the 2008 Fit uses electric power steering rather than a hydraulic system, so there is no separate power-steering fluid to confuse it with.<!-- F-WP02-008 --><!-- WP-12 Section F: ATF leak note --> Green, yellow-green, orange, pink, or blue, with a sweet smell, points to coolant, the fluid that keeps the engine's temperature down.<!-- WP-12 Section F: coolant leak, general automotive knowledge -->

For a smell, describe it in plain words: burning rubber, burning plastic, sweet and syrupy, rotten egg, raw gasoline, or hot oil.<!-- WP-12 Section E: smell categories --> Each points toward a different system, which Chapter 37 explains in full.

### Understanding urgency

Chapter 37's tables each give an urgency level: Low, Medium, Medium-high, or High.<!-- WP-12 Sections C, D, E, F: Urgency column --> Low means the problem is usually just annoying, not dangerous. High means you should stop driving soon, sometimes right away, because the problem could hurt you, hurt the car badly, or both. When you describe a problem to yourself or a shop, always mention anything that feels like a High-urgency sign: a burning smell, a grinding noise while braking, or a flashing warning light.<!-- WP-12 Sections C, E: high-urgency examples -->

### "Fit-specific" versus general knowledge

Some of what you will read in Chapters 35 through 37 comes from real 2007-2008 Honda Fit owners describing their own cars. This book marks those as "Owners report that..." Other information is true of almost any gasoline car and does not need a Fit-specific source, since it is standard automotive knowledge.<!-- WP-12 Sections C-F: "Grounded in" distinction --> Chapter 37 marks each row so you can tell which kind of information you are reading. Neither kind is more or less true. They are simply two different types of evidence.

## Step by step

Use this short routine any time something feels or sounds wrong with your car.

1. Stop and think before you drive further, especially if the problem feels sudden or serious.
2. Note when it happens: at idle, accelerating, turning, braking, or over bumps.<!-- WP-12 Section C -->
3. Note where you notice it: steering wheel, seat and floor, front, middle, or back of the car.<!-- WP-12 Sections C, D -->
4. Note your speed, or your engine speed, when it happens or gets worse.<!-- WP-12 Section D -->
5. Note the color of any fluid, and exactly where under the car it appears.<!-- WP-12 Section F -->
6. Note the smell, using plain words like burning, sweet, or rotten egg.<!-- WP-12 Section E -->
7. Write all of this down, or say it out loud to yourself, before you look anything up.
8. Use your notes with Chapter 35 (check engine light and codes), Chapter 36 (won't start), or Chapter 37 (lookup tables) to narrow down the cause.

## What can go wrong

- **Being too vague.** "It sounds funny" describes nothing. "It ticks at idle, only when the engine is cold" describes something you can look up.
- **Skipping the "when."** A noise that only happens once, right after starting, is very different from a noise that happens constantly. Both matter, but they point to different causes.
- **Guessing the cause instead of describing the symptom.** Say what you heard, felt, saw, or smelled first. Save your guess about the cause for after you check the tables.
- **Ignoring a High-urgency sign because the car "still drives fine."** A car can still move while something inside it is failing badly. Treat a burning smell or a flashing light seriously right away.<!-- WP-12 Section C: flashing MIL example -->
- **Forgetting to check both speed and engine speed for a vibration or a whine.** Road speed and engine speed are not the same thing, and they point to different systems.<!-- WP-12 Section C: alternator whine tied to engine speed, not road speed -->
