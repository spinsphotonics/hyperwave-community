# Chapter 47: Modding Philosophy — Goals, Budget, and the Law

## What you will learn

- Why you should set a clear goal before you buy any part
- What federal law says about your car's emissions equipment
- What a CARB Executive Order (EO) is, and how to look one up
- How modifying your Fit can affect your insurance and your warranty
- What this book will never tell you to do

## The short version

Before you spend money, decide what you want your Fit to do better, and set a budget you will actually keep. Federal law lets you modify your car, but it does not let you remove or defeat any part built to control exhaust emissions. Tell your insurance company about every modification you make, because an untold modification can leave you paying the full bill yourself.

## The full explanation

### Set a goal before you set a budget

**Modding** means changing your car from how the factory built it, usually to improve how it handles, stops, sounds, or looks. Before you buy a single part, decide what you actually want. A car built for **autocross** needs different parts than a car built to look sharp on the street. Autocross is a timed event held in a parking lot or on an airport lot. A car built for daily driving to school needs different parts than either one.

Write your goal down in one sentence. Then set a budget for the whole project, not only the first part. Mods add up fast. A **coilover** kit, an adjustable suspension upgrade covered fully in Chapter 50, is only useful if you can also afford an alignment afterward. Spend on baseline items first, covered in Chapter 48, before you spend on power or looks.

### The law: what "tampering" means

The **Clean Air Act** is the federal law that sets emissions rules for every car sold in the United States, including your Fit. It makes two separate things illegal. First, it is illegal for anyone to manufacture, sell, or install a part built mainly to bypass, defeat, or disable a device that controls your car's exhaust emissions. <!-- F-WP15-001 --> That kind of part is called a **defeat device**, and this rule targets whoever makes, sells, or installs it, not only the driver. <!-- F-WP15-001 -->

Second, a separate part of the same law applies directly to you, the owner. It makes it illegal to knowingly remove or disable any emissions device on your own car after you bought it. <!-- F-WP15-002 --> This second rule is the one that applies to you if you do the work yourself.

Both rules apply everywhere in the United States, not only in states with strict smog laws. <!-- F-WP15-006 --> The federal government, through the EPA (Environmental Protection Agency), enforces them nationwide. **CARB**, the California Air Resources Board, adds its own state-level rules on top of the federal ones, mainly for cars titled or registered in California. <!-- F-WP15-006 --> Chapter 17 explains how to check your own state's inspection and emissions rules.

Here is the good news: EPA does not chase down every driver who installs an **aftermarket** intake or exhaust, meaning a part made by a company other than Honda. <!-- F-WP15-003 --> EPA uses what it calls "enforcement discretion," and it focuses on parts that actually raise emissions. <!-- F-WP15-003 --> EPA generally leaves a part alone if you can show a "reasonable basis" that it will not hurt emissions, proven one of three ways. The part can be identical in design and function to the one it replaces. The modified car can still pass the same emissions tests it was certified with. Or the part can carry a CARB Executive Order that covers your exact vehicle. <!-- F-WP15-003 -->

The penalties for breaking these rules are real money, not a scare tactic. As of the date on EPA's own fact sheet, a manufacturer or dealer can be fined up to $48,192 per violating vehicle or part. <!-- F-WP15-004 --> Any other person, meaning you, can be fined up to $4,819 per violating vehicle or part. <!-- F-WP15-004 --> Faking or disabling emissions-related monitoring equipment, like sensors your check-engine light relies on, is a separate criminal violation on top of that fine. <!-- F-WP15-004 -->

### CARB Executive Orders: the exemption a shop can check

A CARB **Executive Order (EO)** is a certificate California issues for one specific aftermarket part, after CARB's own engineers confirm the part does not raise emissions. <!-- F-WP15-007 --> An EO exempts that exact part, on that exact vehicle, from California's own anti-tampering law. <!-- F-WP15-007 --> A smog-check station or a CARB inspector can look up the EO number to confirm it is real.

Here is the part every reader of this book must remember. One EO does not automatically cover every car the part might physically bolt onto. <!-- F-WP15-008 --> An EO is tied to a specific engine family and a specific model year. <!-- F-WP15-008 --> An EO written for the second-generation 2009-2014 Fit, chassis code GE8, does not automatically cover your 2007-2008 Fit, chassis code GD3, even if the part looks identical on the shelf. <!-- F-WP15-008 --> Always confirm the EO you are reading names your exact model year before you trust it. Chapter 50 walks through this exact year-confusion trap using real suspension parts, so you can see how it plays out with actual products.

You can look up a real EO yourself at CARB's public tool, **https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/aftermarket-parts-lookup**. <!-- F-WP15-009 --> It offers five search categories, and "Performance & Add-on Parts" is the one you want for an intake, header, or exhaust. <!-- F-WP15-009 --> That category page leads into CARB's aftermarket-parts search tool. Outside descriptions say you can search it by EO number, part number, or manufacturer, though this book could not fully confirm every field of that tool works as described. <!-- F-WP15-010 -->

An EO also matters outside California. EPA itself lists "a CARB EO covering the same part" and vehicle as one of the three accepted ways to pass its own federal reasonable-basis test. <!-- F-WP15-011 --> A correctly matched EO is useful evidence anywhere in the country, not only in California. <!-- F-WP15-011 -->

### Warranties and insurance: what new modders forget

EPA's own fact sheet states plainly that tampering "can void manufacturer warranties and insurance agreements." <!-- F-WP15-005 --> That single sentence connects the legal rules above to two very practical costs.

A standard car insurance policy is priced around your car's factory configuration. Any change not made at the factory, whether a suspension part, a **turbocharger** (an engine power upgrade covered fully in Chapter 54), custom paint, or a stereo system, counts as a **modification** for insurance purposes. <!-- F-WP15-012 --> Insurance companies generally will not automatically pay full value for modified parts. To get that coverage, you typically need an added policy **endorsement**, meaning an add-on to your existing policy, sometimes called "custom parts and equipment coverage." <!-- F-WP15-012 --> That coverage only works on top of comprehensive and collision coverage you already carry. <!-- F-WP15-012 -->

If you add parts and never tell your insurance company, the company may refuse to cover those parts. <!-- F-WP15-013 --> You then become responsible for repairing or replacing them yourself, even though you pay for insurance every month. <!-- F-WP15-013 --> Call your insurer before you install anything, not after something breaks.

Not every modification raises your rate. Performance and appearance parts, like a bigger engine output or expensive aftermarket wheels, usually raise cost. They raise the car's value, and they often cost more to repair than factory parts. <!-- F-WP15-014 --> Safety and security additions, like an alarm, a backup camera, or parking sensors, can sometimes earn a discount. This is not guaranteed, though, since some of that added technology is itself expensive to repair. <!-- F-WP15-014 -->

### What this book will never tell you to do

This book never tells you to skip a safety step, and it never tells you to remove or disable emissions equipment for more performance. When a mod could hurt you or someone else, this book says so before it tells you how to do it. If a part looks too good to be true, like a free power gain with no downside, check its real fitment and its real legal status before you buy it. Chapter 50 teaches you exactly how to do that check yourself.
