# Chapter 38: Known Weak Spots on the 2007-2008 Fit

## What you will learn
- What "owners report that" means, and how it is different from a recall.
- Symptoms for 11 wear problems that first-generation Fit owners report.
- A rough shop repair cost for each problem, at today's prices.
- Whether each problem is a good DIY job, or one to leave to a shop.
- The one problem whose repair cost this book could not confirm for the 2008 Fit.

## The short version
Owners of 2007-2008 Fits report 11 wear problems that show up again and again. None of these are recalls. They are patterns spotted by real drivers, not confirmed defects from Honda or the government. Every dollar figure below is a 2024-2026 shop price, not a 2008 price, so an actual 2008 repair bill would have been lower.

## The full explanation

### Why this is different from Chapter 18
Chapter 18 covered recalls and TSBs. A recall is a safety fix Honda must offer for free. A TSB is a repair instruction Honda sends to dealers. Both come from Honda or the government. This chapter is different. It covers patterns that owners on Fit forums have reported seeing again and again on their own cars. This book marks each one with the phrase "owners report that." That phrase means the pattern comes from many individual drivers, not from an official investigation. A community-reported problem can still be real and common. It only means no official body has confirmed it.

### A word about the prices in this chapter
Every dollar amount below comes from repair-cost websites and forum posts checked in 2026. These are today's shop prices, for cars generally 16 to 20 years old. They are not what a repair would have cost when your Fit was new, in 2007 or 2008. Prices also change by region, by shop, and by whether a part is new, aftermarket, or used. Treat every number here as a rough guide, not an exact quote.

### The 11 weak spots

**1. Motor mounts.** Owners report that motor mounts wear out over the life of the car <!-- F-WP02-016 -->. A motor mount is a rubber-and-metal part that bolts the engine and transmission to the car's frame <!-- F-WP02-016 -->. When a mount wears out, you may feel a clunk when you shift into Drive or Reverse <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. You may also feel a clunk under hard acceleration <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. You may feel extra vibration at idle <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. Shop cost for one mount runs roughly $495 to $908, depending on which price page you check <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. New Honda mounts, called OEM parts, cost about $130 each <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. OEM means Original Equipment Manufacturer, the same brand Honda used when it built your car <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. Generic aftermarket mounts run about $30 to $40 each <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. Aftermarket means a part made by a company other than Honda <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. This job is only partly DIY-able <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. DIY means "do it yourself," without paying a shop <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. You need a way to support the engine's weight while a mount is off <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. You also need jack stands and a torque wrench <!-- F-WP13-001 -->. A first-timer should have a helper or a shop manual close by <!-- F-WP13-001 -->.

**2. Timing chain tensioner noise.** Owners report that a ticking or rattling noise comes from the front of the engine <!-- F-WP02-017 -->. It is most noticeable at a cold startup, and it quiets down as the engine warms up <!-- F-WP02-017 -->. A timing chain tensioner keeps the timing chain tight, so the engine's valves open and close at the right moment <!-- F-WP13-002 -->. This is the weakest-sourced item in this chapter <!-- F-WP02-017 -->. Researchers found only one usable online report of this exact problem on a 2008 Fit, not the two separate reports this book normally requires <!-- F-WP02-017 -->. Treat this as a possible pattern, not a settled fact <!-- F-WP02-017 -->. **We could not confirm this for the 2008 Fit.** <!-- F-WP13-002 --> For other small cars, this same kind of tensioner job can run from several hundred dollars to more than a thousand dollars <!-- F-WP13-002 -->. The job costs so much because a mechanic has to remove the front cover of the engine to reach the part <!-- F-WP13-002 -->. This is not a beginner job <!-- F-WP13-002 -->. A chain that slips out of time can bend the engine's valves <!-- F-WP13-002 -->. Take this repair to a shop <!-- F-WP13-002 -->.

**3. Clutch master or slave cylinder wear (manual transmission only).** Owners report that the clutch pedal can suddenly go spongy or soft <!-- F-WP02-018 -->. Sometimes the pedal drops to the floor with little resistance <!-- F-WP02-018 -->. Sometimes there is no fluid puddle under the car, because the leak happens inside the cylinder <!-- F-WP02-018 -->. A master cylinder and a slave cylinder work together to make the clutch disengage when you press the pedal <!-- F-WP13-003 -->. Shops usually replace both cylinders together <!-- F-WP13-003 -->. They do this because the second cylinder tends to fail soon after the first <!-- F-WP13-003 -->. A full job, with both cylinders and a fluid bleed, commonly runs $700 to $1,000 or more <!-- F-WP13-003 -->. This job is only partly DIY-able <!-- F-WP13-003 -->. Swapping the cylinders themselves is not hard <!-- F-WP13-003 -->. Bleeding air out of the hydraulic line afterward is easy to get wrong the first time <!-- F-WP13-003 -->.

**4. Automatic transmission shudder (automatic transmission only).** Owners report that the car shudders, jerks, or hesitates at about 55 to 65 miles per hour on flat ground <!-- F-WP02-019 -->. Forum owners disagree on the cause <!-- F-WP02-019 -->. Some blame the transmission itself <!-- F-WP02-019 -->. Others blame worn spark plugs or ignition coils instead <!-- F-WP02-019 -->. A torque converter is a part inside the automatic transmission that links the spinning engine to the transmission <!-- F-WP13-004 -->. If a worn torque converter turns out to be the cause, expect a bill around $800 to $1,600 <!-- F-WP13-004 -->. This is a general repair-shop price, not a number specific to the Fit <!-- F-WP13-004 -->. Diagnosing this shudder is not a DIY job <!-- F-WP13-004 -->. It needs real test time and, ideally, a scan tool that reads transmission codes <!-- F-WP13-004 -->. Changing the transmission fluid and filter first is a cheap, DIY-able step worth trying before paying for deeper diagnosis <!-- F-WP13-004 -->.

**5. Air conditioning compressor and condenser problems.** Owners report that two separate air conditioning complaints show up on this car <!-- F-WP02-020 -->. First, the condenser can get damaged by road debris <!-- F-WP02-020 -->. A condenser is a radiator-like part, mounted low at the front of the car, that cools the refrigerant <!-- F-WP02-020 -->. One 2008 Fit owner told the government this exact repair cost over $800 <!-- F-WP02-020 -->. Second, some owners report a rattling compressor pulley when the air conditioning turns on <!-- F-WP02-020 -->. Others report air conditioning that cools for a minute and then stops <!-- F-WP02-020 -->. Shop cost for a full compressor replacement runs roughly $1,100 to $1,800, and can run higher <!-- F-WP13-005 -->. This is not a DIY job <!-- F-WP13-005 -->. Opening an air conditioning system needs a special license <!-- F-WP13-005 -->. That license is called EPA Section 609 certification <!-- F-WP13-005 -->. You also need equipment to safely remove the old refrigerant gas <!-- F-WP13-005 -->. A home garage does not have this equipment <!-- F-WP13-005 -->.

**6. Front suspension clunk or knock.** Owners report that a metallic clunk or knock comes from the front of the car over bumps <!-- F-WP02-021 -->. Forum owners most often trace this to one of two parts <!-- F-WP02-021 -->. The first is a worn sway bar end link <!-- F-WP02-021 -->. A sway bar end link is a small linking part that connects the sway bar to the suspension <!-- F-WP02-021 -->. The second is a worn strut top mount <!-- F-WP02-021 -->. A strut top mount is the rubber bushing where the top of a strut bolts to the car's body <!-- F-WP02-021 -->. Owner advice is to check the end links first, before paying for a full strut job <!-- F-WP02-021 -->. Sway bar end links cost roughly $75 to $200 to replace on one side, or $200 to $300 to do both sides at once <!-- F-WP13-006 -->. A full pair of struts, with new top mounts, runs roughly $400 to $900 <!-- F-WP13-006 -->. End links are a real beginner DIY job <!-- F-WP13-006 -->. The job needs only a floor jack, jack stands, and hand tools <!-- F-WP13-006 -->. Struts need a spring compressor tool and real caution <!-- F-WP13-006 -->. A released spring can badly hurt you <!-- F-WP13-006 -->.

**7. Dashboard or glovebox rattle.** Owners report that a rattle comes from the dashboard or glovebox area over bumps <!-- F-WP02-022 -->. It is most often traced to a loose plastic panel under the windshield <!-- F-WP02-022 -->. Sometimes it is a part behind the factory radio instead <!-- F-WP02-022 -->. The rattle tends to get worse in cold weather <!-- F-WP02-022 -->. There is no set price for this job, since it depends on finding the cause first <!-- F-WP13-007 -->. Shops typically bill it as one diagnostic fee visit <!-- F-WP13-007 -->. A diagnostic fee is the charge for the time a shop spends finding a problem before fixing it <!-- F-WP13-007 -->. That fee runs roughly $50 to $150 at an independent shop <!-- F-WP13-007 -->. It can run $100 to $400 or more at a dealer <!-- F-WP13-007 -->. Once found, the actual fix often costs under $20 in parts, such as a broken clip <!-- F-WP13-007 -->. This is a good DIY job for a patient beginner <!-- F-WP13-007 -->. It needs only basic plastic trim-removal tools <!-- F-WP13-007 -->.

**8. Clearcoat peeling.** Owners report that the clearcoat flakes or peels on flat, horizontal surfaces after several years <!-- F-WP02-023 -->. The roof and hood are the surfaces owners mention most <!-- F-WP02-023 -->. Clearcoat is the clear, protective top layer of a car's paint <!-- F-WP02-023 -->. Owners, and a paint-repair professional in one forum discussion, blame thin or improperly cured factory clearcoat <!-- F-WP02-023 -->. Fixing one panel, like only the roof, commonly costs $500 to $1,500 or more <!-- F-WP13-008 -->. A full repaint is sometimes needed once the damage has spread across several panels <!-- F-WP13-008 -->. A full repaint can run $2,000 to $5,000, and sometimes over $10,000 <!-- F-WP13-008 -->. This is not a DIY job <!-- F-WP13-008 -->. Matching factory paint color takes real skill <!-- F-WP13-008 -->. Spraying an even clearcoat without runs also takes real skill and equipment <!-- F-WP13-008 -->.

**9. Rust at the rear quarter panel and wheel well.** Owners report that rust starts where the rear quarter panel meets the rear bumper cover <!-- F-WP02-024 -->. It also starts along a hidden seam near the rocker panel and wheel well <!-- F-WP02-024 -->. This spot collects road grit and moisture <!-- F-WP02-024 -->. Left alone, it can eat through to holes in the driver's rear wheel well <!-- F-WP02-024 -->. This mostly affects cars driven where roads get salted in winter <!-- F-WP02-024 -->. Cleaning and treating surface rust early costs roughly $150 to $900 <!-- F-WP13-009 -->. A real patch repair, once metal has to be cut and welded, runs roughly $800 to $1,800 <!-- F-WP13-009 -->. A full panel replacement can run up to about $2,500 <!-- F-WP13-009 -->. Catching rust early is a real DIY job <!-- F-WP13-009 -->. It uses a rust converter product you can buy cheaply <!-- F-WP13-009 -->. Welding in a new patch panel needs skill and equipment most beginners do not have <!-- F-WP13-009 -->.

**10. Battery drain.** Owners report that the battery goes dead after sitting for two or three days <!-- F-WP02-025 -->. Some owners report the battery draining overnight instead <!-- F-WP02-025 -->. Reported causes include a faulty door switch, an electrical short, or an aftermarket add-on wired into the fuse box, such as an alarm <!-- F-WP02-025 -->. One technical forum post says a healthy 2008 Fit should draw only about 15 to 17 milliamps with everything off <!-- F-WP02-025 -->. A milliamp, written mA, is a small unit of electric current <!-- F-WP02-025 -->. A draw above roughly 40 to 50 mA points to a real fault worth chasing <!-- F-WP02-025 -->. Basic diagnosis at a shop runs $50 to $100 <!-- F-WP13-010 -->. Tracing a harder short can run $150 to $400 or more <!-- F-WP13-010 -->. After that, a new battery costs $50 to $200 <!-- F-WP13-010 -->. A new alternator runs $200 to $600 <!-- F-WP13-010 -->. A wiring repair starts around $100 and climbs with complexity <!-- F-WP13-010 -->. The diagnostic step is DIY-able with a simple multimeter and patience <!-- F-WP13-010 -->. Replacing a battery or a simple switch is beginner-easy <!-- F-WP13-010 -->. Chasing an intermittent wiring short is much harder <!-- F-WP13-010 -->.

**11. Weak factory radio and speakers.** Owners report that the factory speaker system sounds thin <!-- F-WP02-026 -->. Some trims are missing rear door speakers <!-- F-WP02-026 -->. Some head units lack a way to plug in an aftermarket amplifier <!-- F-WP02-026 -->. This is a comfort complaint, not a safety defect <!-- F-WP02-026 -->. A professional head-unit swap commonly costs $150 to $550 all-in for a basic unit <!-- F-WP13-011 -->. A unit with Bluetooth or a phone-mirroring screen costs more <!-- F-WP13-011 -->. Replacing all four speakers plus the head unit at a shop runs roughly $600 to $1,100 <!-- F-WP13-011 -->. This is one of the most beginner-friendly car projects there is <!-- F-WP13-011 -->. It needs only a screwdriver and a trim-panel tool <!-- F-WP13-011 -->. A wiring adapter is a good idea too, so you do not have to cut the factory wiring <!-- F-WP13-011 -->.

### Putting it together
Most of these 11 items are wear-and-tear, not danger <!-- F-WP02-016 -->. None of them stop you from driving your car safely on their own <!-- F-WP02-016 -->. Still, always fix a real safety issue, like a shuddering transmission or a suspension clunk, before it gets worse <!-- F-WP02-019 -->. The next chapter shows you how to find a fair shop for any repair on this list that you cannot, or should not, do yourself.
