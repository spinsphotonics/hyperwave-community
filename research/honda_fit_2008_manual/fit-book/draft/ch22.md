# Chapter 22: Tools and Supplies for a Starter Garage

## What you will learn

- Every tool this book's maintenance chapters actually call for
- What each tool does, in plain words, the first time it comes up
- Which tools you need for almost every job, and which ones are for bigger jobs only
- A sensible order to buy tools in, so you are not buying everything at once
- Safety gear and shop supplies to keep on hand besides tools

## The short version

This chapter lists only the tools named in this book's own maintenance procedures, nothing extra. You do not need a full mechanic's toolbox to start. A handful of tools cover most jobs, and a few more only matter once you tackle brakes or oil changes.

## The full explanation

### Turning and tightening tools

Most jobs in this book start with turning a bolt. A **ratchet** is a handle that turns a socket, a metal cap that fits over a bolt head, in one direction without you lifting it off between turns. A **breaker bar** is a plain, longer handle with no ratcheting action, used when a bolt is stuck and needs extra force. <!-- P-WP11-1 --> You need a ratchet or breaker bar with sockets sized to your car's bolts for the oil drain plug and for many other jobs. <!-- P-WP11-1 -->

A **torque wrench** is a tool that tells you exactly how hard you are tightening a bolt, by feel or by a dial. It measures in pound-feet (lbf·ft) or newton-meters (N·m). <!-- P-WP11-1 --> You need one that reaches at least 80 lbf·ft (108 N·m), the torque for your wheel nuts. <!-- F-WP10-001 --> The same torque wrench should also reach down to 29 lbf·ft (39 N·m), the torque for your oil drain bolt. <!-- F-WP10-002, P-WP11-1 --> Guessing at torque by feel alone risks a leak or a stripped bolt, so this tool is worth buying early.

An **oil filter wrench** is a tool shaped to grip the round oil filter so you can twist it loose. The owner's manual itself calls this "a special wrench, available from your dealer." <!-- P-WP11-1 --> You also need a **lug wrench**, or a socket that fits your wheel nuts plus a breaker bar, any time you take a wheel off. <!-- P-WP11-5 --> A **flat-tip (flathead) screwdriver** and a **Phillips screwdriver** cover the clips and screws on your air filter housing. <!-- P-WP11-2, P-WP11-3 -->

### Lifting and support tools

A **floor jack** is a tool on wheels that lifts one corner or one end of the car using a hand pump or lever. <!-- P-WP11-22 --> Buy one rated for at least your car's weight. The small scissor jack that lives in your trunk is only rated for a roadside flat tire, not for working underneath the car. <!-- P-WP11-22 -->

**Jack stands** are metal supports you slide under the car once it is lifted, so the car's weight rests on solid steel instead of the jack. <!-- P-WP11-22 --> Buy at least two, and ideally four, each rated for at least your car's weight. <!-- P-WP11-1, P-WP11-5, P-WP11-22 --> Chapter 23 covers how to use a jack and jack stands safely, and that chapter's warnings apply every time you use these tools.

**Wheel chocks** are wedges you place against a tire that stays on the ground, so the car cannot roll while another corner is lifted. <!-- P-WP11-5, P-WP11-22 --> You need at least two.

### Tire and brake tools

A **tire pressure gauge** measures the air pressure inside a tire, in pounds per square inch (psi). <!-- P-WP11-4 --> The owner's manual recommends using your own gauge every time, instead of a different shop gauge each visit, so your readings stay consistent. <!-- P-WP11-4 --> You will also want a portable inflator, or access to a gas station air pump, to add air. <!-- P-WP11-4 -->

A **flashlight** lets you see the brake pad through the wheel spokes without removing the wheel; a ruler or a dedicated pad-thickness gauge is a helpful, optional extra. <!-- P-WP11-6 -->

Once you move on to replacing brake pads, a **C-clamp**, or a dedicated brake piston tool, pushes the caliper piston back into its housing to make room for new pads. <!-- P-WP11-7 --> Rear drum brakes need their own set of tools. Use large C-clamps or brake-spring pliers to compress the brake shoes, plus a drum brake spring and hold-down tool kit to handle the springs and pins safely. <!-- P-WP11-8 --> A **wire brush** and **brake-parts cleaner**, a solvent spray made for cleaning brake dust, are needed for both front pad and rear drum jobs. <!-- P-WP11-7, P-WP11-8 -->

Bleeding your brakes means pushing old fluid and trapped air out of the brake lines. <!-- P-WP11-9 --> For that job, you need a wrench sized to the small **bleeder screw** on each brake, clear plastic tubing that fits snugly over it, and a clean catch bottle for the old fluid. <!-- P-WP11-9 --> A turkey baster or a fluid syringe helps remove old fluid from the reservoir before you start. <!-- P-WP11-9 -->

### Safety gear and shop supplies

Nitrile gloves and safety glasses protect your hands and eyes any time you work with oil, fresh or used. <!-- P-WP11-1 --> Keep a stack of shop rags on hand for wiping up drips and cleaning parts. <!-- P-WP11-1 --> You will also want a sealed container to carry used oil to a recycling center, since used oil cannot go in the trash or on the ground. <!-- P-WP11-1 --> For brake work, keep high-temperature brake grease on hand for pad backing and slide pins. <!-- P-WP11-7 --> For the parking-brake lever pivot on drum brakes, use a specific product called Molykote 44MA, or an equivalent high-temperature brake grease. <!-- P-WP11-8 -->

Chapters 24 through 27 name the exact oil, filters, pads, shoes, and fluid your car needs. This chapter only covers the tools that install them.

## Step by step: Building your toolkit in a sensible order

You do not need every tool on day one. Buy in this order, based on which jobs come first in this book.

1. Buy a tire pressure gauge first. It is cheap, and Chapter 26 asks you to check your tires often. <!-- P-WP11-4 -->
2. Add nitrile gloves, safety glasses, and shop rags. Every job after this uses at least one of them. <!-- P-WP11-1 -->
3. Add a ratchet or breaker bar with a socket set, a lug wrench, and a flashlight. These cover inspection jobs and light repairs. <!-- P-WP11-1, P-WP11-5, P-WP11-6 -->
4. Before your first oil change, add a torque wrench, an oil filter wrench, a drain pan, and a funnel. <!-- P-WP11-1 -->
5. Before you lift the car for any reason, add a floor jack, jack stands, and wheel chocks, and read Chapter 23 first. <!-- P-WP11-22 -->
6. Before a brake job, add the C-clamp or piston tool, wire brush, brake-parts cleaner, and, for rear drums, the spring and hold-down tool kit. <!-- P-WP11-7, P-WP11-8 -->
7. Before bleeding brakes, add the bleeder wrench, clear tubing, a catch bottle, and a turkey baster or syringe. <!-- P-WP11-9 -->

## What can go wrong

Several procedure cards behind this book flag one bolt size or another as unconfirmed, telling you to check your own car before buying a socket rather than guess. <!-- P-WP11-1, P-WP11-7, P-WP11-9 --> Buying the wrong socket size wastes a trip to the store, so measure or check the bolt on your own car first when a size is not given. A torque wrench is not optional if a job names a torque number. Tightening by feel alone can leave a bolt too loose, which can fail later, or too tight, which can strip threads or crack a part. <!-- F-WP10-001, F-WP10-002 --> Do not swap a floor jack alone for jack stands. The floor jack lifts the car, but only the jack stands are meant to hold its weight while you work underneath. <!-- P-WP11-22 -->
