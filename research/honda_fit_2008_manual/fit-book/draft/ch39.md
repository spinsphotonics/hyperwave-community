# Chapter 39: When to Go to a Shop, and How to Not Get Ripped Off

## What you will learn
- The difference between an independent shop and a dealership, and why their prices differ.
- Typical hourly labor rates for each, at today's prices.
- Three situations where paying a dealer's higher rate still makes sense.
- How to read every line of a repair estimate before you agree to pay.
- Simple habits that protect you from a surprise bill.

## The short version
Independent shops usually charge less per hour than dealerships. A dealer is still the right choice in a few specific cases. Every repair estimate has the same five parts: a diagnostic fee, labor, parts, shop supplies, and tax. Read every line before you agree to a repair, and compare the final bill to that estimate before you pay.

## The full explanation

### Independent shop versus dealership: labor rates
A labor rate is the hourly price a shop charges for a mechanic's time <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. It is separate from the cost of parts <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. An independent shop is a repair shop not owned by a car maker <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. Independent shops commonly charge $80 to $130 per hour <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. Dealerships commonly charge $120 to $180 per hour <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. A separate national study found that nearly half of all US repair shops price their labor somewhere between $120 and $159 per hour <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. Dealerships tend to charge more for a few reasons <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. Their mechanics go through the car maker's own training programs <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. They must buy diagnostic tools the maker requires <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. They also carry higher costs from running a franchise and a larger building <!-- F-WP13-012 -->.

For a routine job you already understand, like brakes or a motor mount, an independent shop can often do equal-quality work for meaningfully less money <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. Look for a shop with mechanics who hold ASE certification, a widely recognized test for mechanics <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. A dealer becomes the better choice in three cases <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. First, the repair needs a special tool only the maker sells <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. Second, the repair is still covered by a warranty, a recall, or a TSB; see Chapter 18 for your car's own recall list <!-- F-WP13-012 -->. Third, the needed part is sold only through the dealer's own parts network <!-- F-WP13-012 -->.

### How to read a repair estimate
Ask for a written estimate before any work starts <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. Do not settle for a spoken number only <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A complete estimate has five parts <!-- F-WP13-013 -->.

**Diagnostic fee.** This is the charge for the time a shop spends figuring out what is wrong <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. It comes before any repair is agreed to <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. It commonly covers 60 to 90 minutes of labor <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. Many shops apply this fee toward your repair bill if you have the work done there <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. Ask this question before you say yes to the diagnosis <!-- F-WP13-013 -->.

**Labor.** This line shows the job name, a number of hours, and the shop's hourly rate <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. Hours are often written in tenths, so 3.5 means three and a half hours <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. You can check the math yourself by multiplying the hours by the rate <!-- F-WP13-013 -->.

**Parts.** Each parts line should say exactly what is being replaced <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. It should also say what kind of part it is <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A part can be new OEM, meaning Original Equipment Manufacturer, the same brand Honda used <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A part can be new aftermarket, made by a different company <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A part can be remanufactured, meaning a used part rebuilt to work like new <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A part can also be plain used <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A cheaper price on this line often means a lower-tier part, which is worth asking about <!-- F-WP13-013 -->.

**Shop supplies.** This line covers small extra costs, like rags, cleaning fluid, and small fasteners <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. It is normal to see this charge <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. It is usually about 5 to 10 percent of the labor total <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A vague fee, with no clear dollar amount or percentage, is worth questioning <!-- F-WP13-013 -->.

**Tax.** Sales tax applies to parts in most states <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. Whether labor also gets taxed depends on your state <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. This line will vary by where you live <!-- F-WP13-013 -->.

### Habits that protect you
Get every estimate in writing before work starts <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. Check that the paperwork lists your own car's VIN and mileage, not another car's <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A VIN is your car's own 17-character serial number, covered in Chapter 18 <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. When the final bill arrives, compare it line by line against the original estimate <!-- F-WP13-013 -->. A bill that grew without a phone call explaining why is a warning sign <!-- F-WP13-013 -->.

### Tying it back to Chapter 38
Chapter 38 told you which of the 11 weak spots a beginner can safely try at home. When a repair on that list is marked "no," or needs tools you do not have, use this chapter's habits before you pay. Ask for a written estimate. Read every line. Compare the final bill against it. Doing this each time protects you far more than trying to memorize a "fair price" for every possible repair.
