# Chapter 41: Suspension: struts, shocks, sway bar links, bushings

## What you will learn

- Why the front struts are a shop job, but the rear shocks are not
- What a shop will do when it replaces your front struts
- How to replace your rear shocks yourself, step by step
- How to replace your front sway bar end links and rear stabilizer bushing
- The difference between an end-link swap and a full sway bar replacement
- What can go wrong on each of these jobs

## The short version

Your Fit's suspension holds the car up, controls its bounce, and keeps the tires planted on bumps. Some suspension parts are easy weekend jobs, and some are not. <!-- P-WP14-3 --> <!-- P-WP14-4 --> <!-- P-WP14-5 --> The front struts need a spring compressor and carry a real injury risk, so this book treats that one as a shop job. <!-- P-WP14-3 --> The rear shocks, the front sway bar end links, and the rear stabilizer bushing are all simpler jobs you can do yourself with basic tools and jack stands. <!-- P-WP14-4 --> <!-- P-WP14-5 -->

## The full explanation

A strut and a shock absorber, often called a "shock" for short, do the same basic job: they slow down your suspension's bounce after a bump. A sway bar, also called a stabilizer bar, is a metal bar that connects the suspension on the car's left side to its right side. It reduces how much the body leans in turns. <!-- P-WP14-5 --> A sway bar end link is a short linkage part that connects the end of the sway bar to the suspension. A bushing is a rubber or rubber-and-metal piece that cushions a joint and lets it flex slightly. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->

Your Fit's front suspension uses a MacPherson strut, a design where a coil spring wraps directly around the strut itself. <!-- P-WP14-3 --> That spring stores a large amount of energy while compressed. A tool called a spring compressor squeezes the spring so a mechanic can safely take the strut apart. If a compressor slips or is used the wrong way, it can release that stored energy suddenly and cause serious injury or death. <!-- P-WP14-3 --> This book could not confirm the exact top-nut torque number for this car's front strut from two independent sources. That gap is one more reason this book sends the front strut job to a shop, instead of guessing at that number. <!-- P-WP14-3 -->

Your Fit's rear suspension is different. The rear coil spring sits on its own in the rear axle beam, not wrapped around the shock. That means the rear shock job needs no spring compressor at all, which makes it a meaningfully easier job than the front strut. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->

This chapter also covers two smaller jobs that people often lump together under "sway bar links" and "bushings": swapping the front end links, and swapping the rear stabilizer bushing. Replacing the sway bar itself, the long bar rather than its end links, is a much bigger job on this car. It requires dropping the front subframe and the steering gearbox. This book treats a full sway bar swap as a shop job, and does not give full steps for it here. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->

## What the shop will do: front struts

Even if a shop does this job, know what to ask about, before you read the summary below. Always treat a coil spring on a strut as if it stores enough energy to cause serious injury if it lets go suddenly and out of control. <!-- P-WP14-3 --> Never let anyone attempt this job with a homemade spring compressor, such as rope, chain, or a bar clamp; a real job uses a purpose-built strut spring compressor. <!-- P-WP14-3 --> Always ask the shop to check, and if needed perform, a wheel alignment after the strut job. <!-- P-WP14-3 -->

A shop replacing your front struts will typically:

- Raise the car and remove the front wheel on each side. <!-- P-WP14-3 -->
- Disconnect the strut from the steering knuckle, then unbolt the brake hose bracket and any ABS sensor wiring clip from the strut. <!-- P-WP14-3 -->
- Remove the three top strut-mount nuts from inside the engine bay and lift the strut and spring out as one assembly. <!-- P-WP14-3 -->
- On a workbench, compress the spring with a proper spring compressor, then remove the top-mount nut while holding the shaft still with a hex wrench. Take the strut apart and rebuild it with the new part. <!-- P-WP14-3 -->
- Reinstall the new strut assembly in reverse order and torque every fastener to the specification in the shop's own service information. <!-- P-WP14-3 -->
- Perform a wheel alignment afterward. New struts can change ride height slightly. That can shift camber, how far the top of a tire tilts in or out, or toe, how far the tires point toward or away from each other. <!-- P-WP14-3 -->

## Step by step

### Rear shocks (both sides)

Never work under a car held up only by a jack; always use jack stands. Use a floor jack only to support the axle beam while you work, not as your only support. <!-- P-WP14-4 --> Always wear eye protection when working under the car. <!-- P-WP14-4 --> Never let the shock swing loose and strike the fuel tank, brake lines, or wiring; keep control of its movement as you remove or install it. <!-- P-WP14-4 --> Always take care not to scratch the car's body while maneuvering the shock in and out of its mounting spot. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->

1. Raise the rear of the car, support it on jack stands, and remove both rear wheels. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
2. Remove the wire guide grommet from both sides of the rear axle beam, the metal beam that the rear wheels attach to. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
3. Position a floor jack under the lower spring seat on the axle beam. Raise it slightly, only enough for the suspension to begin compressing. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
4. Remove the damper mounting bolt that connects the axle beam to the bottom of the shock. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
5. Remove the access panel from the cargo area's side trim to reach the shock's top mount. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
6. Hold the shock shaft still with a hex wrench, and remove the self-locking top nut. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
7. Remove the top mounting washer and rubber mount. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
8. Compress the shock by hand and lift it out of the car, taking care not to scratch the body. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
9. Repeat steps 1 through 8 on the other side, if you are replacing both shocks. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
10. Fit the rubber mount onto the new shock's top, and position the shock between the body and the axle beam. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
11. Raise the floor jack slowly until the bottom mounting hole lines up. Loosely fit the new bottom mounting bolt. Honda's own procedure calls for a new bolt here, not the old one. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
12. Raise the axle beam with the jack until the car's rear barely lifts off the jack stands, then torque the bottom mounting bolt to 40 lbf-ft (54 N-m). <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
13. Fit the top rubber mount, washer, and a new self-locking nut onto the shock shaft. Honda's own procedure calls for a new nut here too. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
14. Hold the shaft still with the hex wrench and tighten the top nut. We could not confirm the exact torque number for this nut on this pass; check Appendix C or your new shock's instructions for it. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
15. Reinstall the access panel, the wire guide grommets, and both rear wheels. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->
16. Lower the car and torque the wheel nuts to 80 lbf-ft (108 N-m) in a crisscross pattern. <!-- P-WP14-4 -->

### Front sway bar end links (both sides)

Never work under a car held up only by a jack; always use jack stands. <!-- P-WP14-5 --> Always wear eye protection, especially when you use penetrating oil or a cutting tool on rusted fasteners. <!-- P-WP14-5 --> Never use a cutting wheel near brake lines, fuel lines, or wiring; check what is nearby before you cut a stuck fastener. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->

1. Raise the front of the car and support it on jack stands, with both front wheels off the ground. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
2. Spray the end-link nuts with penetrating oil and let it sit for a few minutes. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
3. Hold the end-link stud still with a hex key while loosening the nut with a wrench. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
4. If the stud spins instead of the nut, grip the stud's shoulder with vice grips. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
5. If a nut is fully seized, cut it off carefully with a cutting wheel rather than forcing it loose. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
6. Remove the old end link from both its top connection and its bottom connection to the sway bar. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
7. Fit the new end link, and start both nuts by hand. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
8. Check the new part's instructions for the suspension position it wants during tightening: unloaded, with the wheel off the ground, or loaded to normal ride height. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
9. Position the suspension the way the instructions call for, then tighten both nuts snug. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
10. Repeat steps 2 through 9 on the other side. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
11. Lower the car and check that both end links move freely as the suspension travels. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->

### Rear stabilizer bushing

Never work under a car held up only by a jack; always use jack stands. <!-- P-WP14-5 --> Always cover nearby electrical connectors before using silicone spray, since silicone residue can interfere with switches and sensors. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->

1. Raise the rear of the car and support it on jack stands. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
2. Remove the old stabilizer bushing from the axle beam. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
3. Apply silicone spray to the new bushing to ease its installation. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
4. Line up the new bushing's end face with the center of its mounting hole, and press it into the axle beam. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
5. Make sure the stabilizer bar seats fully into the bushing's groove, and does not stick out past the end of the axle beam. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->

## What can go wrong

Skipping the alignment check after a strut job can lead to uneven tire wear later. <!-- P-WP14-3 --> Reusing a worn or cracked top-mount bearing instead of replacing it along with the strut is a common shortcut that causes new noises. <!-- P-WP14-3 --> On the rear shocks, not supporting the axle beam with a floor jack before removing the bottom bolt can let the beam drop suddenly. <!-- P-WP14-4 --> Reusing the old self-locking top nut or bottom bolt, instead of the new ones Honda's procedure calls for, is another common mistake. <!-- P-WP14-4 --> On the sway bar end links, forcing a seized nut without penetrating oil first can snap the stud. Tightening the links with the suspension hanging free, instead of at normal ride height, can pre-load the rubber wrong. <!-- P-WP14-5 --> On the rear bushing, getting silicone spray on nearby electrical connectors can cause switch problems later. <!-- P-WP14-5 --> Finally, do not confuse a noisy or loose end link with a problem in the sway bar's own mounting bushings on the subframe. That bigger issue means the full bar has to come out, which is the shop-recommended job, not the end-link swap in this chapter. <!-- P-WP14-5 -->
