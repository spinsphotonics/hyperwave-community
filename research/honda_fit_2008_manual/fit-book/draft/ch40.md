# Chapter 40: Brake job, full

## What you will learn

- The difference between a basic pad or shoe swap and a full brake job
- How to check a front brake rotor and replace it along with the pads
- How to check a rear brake drum and replace or resurface it
- The extra tools a full brake job needs beyond a basic pad or shoe job
- What can go wrong on these harder steps, and how to catch it

## The short version

Chapter 27 already showed you how to swap front brake pads and rear brake shoes on their own. <!-- P-WP11-7 --> <!-- P-WP11-8 --> A full brake job goes one step further: you also check the front rotor and the rear drum, and replace either one if it is worn out. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> <!-- P-WP14-2 --> You do this work with the same jack stands, the same care, and mostly the same tools as Chapter 27, plus a few extras for the rotor and the drum.

## The full explanation

A rotor is the flat metal disc that spins with your front wheel. The caliper squeezes your brake pads against it to slow the car. A drum is the metal cylinder that spins with your rear wheel. Your brake shoes press outward against the inside of the drum to slow the car. <!-- P-WP11-8 -->

Pads and shoes wear out on their own schedule, and so do rotors and drums. A rotor or drum can wear thin, get scored with deep grooves, or crack, even while the pads or shoes next to it still have life left. Both the rotor and the drum carry a wear-limit number stamped into the metal. If your measurement goes past that number, or the metal is cracked or deeply grooved, you replace the part instead of reusing it. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> <!-- P-WP14-2 -->

This chapter does not repeat the pad and shoe steps from Chapter 27. It focuses only on what a full brake job adds: taking the rotor or drum off, checking it, and putting a new or resurfaced one back on.

## Step by step

### Front brakes: pads and rotors together

Do this job with the safety rules from Chapter 27 in mind. Always wear eye protection, since brake dust and rust flakes can hurt your eyes. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Do not breathe brake dust, and never use compressed air or a dry brush on brake parts; use brake-parts cleaner instead. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Never work under a car held up only by a jack; always use jack stands. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Work on one wheel at a time and leave the other wheel on the ground, so you always have a reference for how the parts go back together. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Never let the caliper hang by its brake hose; support it so the hose carries no weight. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Never resurface or reuse a rotor that is cracked, deeply grooved, or thinner than its stamped minimum thickness; replace it instead. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Always pump the brake pedal several times before you drive, until it feels firm again. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->

1. Loosen the front wheel nuts, then raise and support the car on jack stands, and remove the wheel. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
2. Remove the caliper slide-pin bolts and lift the caliper off its bracket. Hang the caliper so it does not pull on the brake hose. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
3. Remove the two caliper mounting-bracket bolts and lift the bracket off the steering knuckle, the metal part the wheel bearing sits in. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
4. Check the rotor's edge for a minimum-thickness number stamped into the metal, and write it down before you remove the rotor. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
5. If the rotor is stuck to the hub, tap it near its center with a rubber mallet. Never hit the braking surface. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
6. Pull the old rotor off the hub studs. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
7. Clean rust and debris off the hub face with a wire brush, then wipe it clean. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
8. Apply a thin coat of anti-seize compound to the hub face only. Keep it off the braking surface. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
9. Slide the new rotor onto the hub studs. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
10. Clean the caliper bracket and slide pins with a wire brush and brake-parts cleaner. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
11. Reinstall the caliper bracket. Owners report torquing its mounting bolts to 80 lbf-ft (108 N-m), but this number is not independently confirmed for this car. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
12. Use a C-clamp against an old pad, or a block of wood, to push the caliper piston back into its bore. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
13. Coat the new pads' metal backing and the slide pins with a thin layer of brake grease, then place the pads into the bracket. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
14. Slide the caliper back over the new pads and reinstall the slide-pin bolts. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
15. Reinstall the wheel, snug the nuts by hand, then lower the car and torque the wheel nuts to 80 lbf-ft (108 N-m) in a crisscross pattern. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
16. Repeat steps 1 through 15 on the other front wheel. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
17. Pump the brake pedal several times with the engine off, until it feels firm. Then check the brake fluid reservoir and top it off if needed. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->
18. Drive slowly at first and test the brakes gently in an empty area before you return to normal driving. <!-- P-WP14-1 -->

### Rear brakes: drums

Chapter 27 already walked you through the rear brake shoe swap on this car, including the return spring, which is the hardest single step in that job. <!-- P-WP11-8 --> This section only adds the drum check. Do it in the same session as the shoe swap, since the drum has to come off to reach the shoes anyway. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->

Always wear eye protection when you inspect or clean a drum. <!-- P-WP14-2 --> Never reinstall a drum that is cracked, deeply scored, or worn past its stamped maximum diameter; replace it instead. <!-- P-WP14-2 --> Never work under a car held up only by a jack; always use jack stands. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->

1. Follow the first three steps of Chapter 27's rear brake job: raise the car, remove the wheel, and pull the drum off the hub. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->
2. Look for a maximum-diameter number cast or stamped into the drum's outer face. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->
3. Measure the drum's inside diameter with a micrometer or caliper, checking several points around the circle. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->
4. If any measurement goes past the stamped maximum diameter, or the surface has deep grooves or cracks, plan to replace both rear drums as a pair. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->
5. If the drum is within its limit but glazed or lightly scored, a machine shop can resurface it instead of replacing it. Ask the shop whether resurfacing costs less than a new drum. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->
6. If you are replacing the drum, do not transfer anything from the old drum itself. Only the shoes, springs, and adjuster carry over. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->
7. Finish the rest of Chapter 27's rear brake shoe steps, using the new or resurfaced drum in place of the old one. <!-- P-WP14-2 -->

## What can go wrong

Getting anti-seize compound or grease on the rotor's braking surface, or on the pad linings, causes squealing and cuts your stopping power. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Reusing a rotor or drum that is warped, cracked, or past its wear limit is the most common mistake in a full brake job. Always check the stamped number before you decide to reuse a part. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> <!-- P-WP14-2 --> Pushing a caliper piston back without first loosening the brake fluid reservoir cap can push fluid out or damage a seal. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Letting a caliper hang by its hose can stretch or kink the hose. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> Replacing only one side's drum, instead of both as a pair, can cause the car to pull or brake unevenly. <!-- P-WP14-2 --> And skipping the "pump the pedal first" step, on either job, can mean the pedal goes soft or all the way to the floor on your first drive. <!-- P-WP14-1 --> <!-- P-WP14-2 -->
