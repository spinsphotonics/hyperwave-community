# Chapter 20: Buying a Used 2008 Fit: Inspection Checklist and Known Trouble Spots

## What you will learn
- What a general **pre-purchase inspection (PPI)** covers on any used car.
- How to fold the 8 recalls from Chapter 18 into your buying checklist.
- The one service bulletin worth asking about before you buy.
- Eight trouble spots owners report on first-generation Fits, and how to check each one on a test drive.
- What a used 2008 Fit tends to cost in today's market, in a clear price table.
- Why two of the pricing sources disagree, and what a typical odometer reading looks like at this car's age.

## The short version
Before you buy a used 2008 Fit, run its VIN through the recall check from Chapter 18 and get a mechanic's inspection. Owners report eight specific wear spots on this car, so check each one on the test drive. Expect a wide price range depending on the source and the car's condition. Expect the odometer to show anywhere from about 100,000 to 250,000 or more miles.

## The full explanation

### A general pre-purchase inspection
A **pre-purchase inspection**, or **PPI**, is a check of a used car before you buy it, done by you or by a paid independent mechanic <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. A general PPI covers five main areas on any car <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. First, the exterior and body: look for crash damage, mismatched paint, rust, and signs of flood damage <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. Also check whether the car sits level, and check for cracked or chipped glass <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. Second, tires and brakes: check tread depth, the tire's age code on the sidewall, uneven wear, and the condition of the pads, rotors, and calipers <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. Third, the engine bay and the underside: check for fluid leaks, worn belts and hoses, and rust on the frame <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. A puddle under a parked car is worth investigating, and a transmission leak is especially expensive to ignore <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. Fourth, the interior and electronics: test the heat and air conditioning, and check the instrument cluster and warning lights <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. Confirm every gauge and light actually works <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. Fifth, a test drive that checks braking, steering feel, suspension, and acceleration <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. Also ask the mechanic to scan the car's computer for stored **diagnostic trouble codes (DTCs)** <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. A code can be cleared right before a sale, only to come back after you have driven the car for a while <!-- F-WP08-010 -->. A professional PPI from an independent shop commonly costs about $100 to $200 <!-- F-WP08-010 -->.

### Confirm every recall is closed
Run the car's VIN through the recall check described in Chapter 18 before you buy <!-- F-WP08-011 -->. Confirm all 8 recalls from that chapter's table show as completed, not open <!-- F-WP08-011 -->. Pay special attention to two of them <!-- F-WP08-011 -->. The drive shaft recall (20V770000) only applies if the car has a manual transmission <!-- F-WP08-011 -->. The ODS wire harness recall (07V549000) only applies if the car was ever sold or registered in a salt-belt state <!-- F-WP08-011 -->. Do not be alarmed if a general web search turns up campaign 19V182000, since that recall names only the 2007 Fit, not your 2008 Fit <!-- F-WP08-011 -->. Trust what the VIN search itself shows for your specific car, not a general search <!-- F-WP08-011 -->.

### Ask about the steering bulletin too
Ask the seller whether the check engine light has ever come on with a steering-related code <!-- F-WP08-012 -->. Also ask whether the steering has ever felt unusually heavy, especially when the car is stopped <!-- F-WP08-012 -->. This relates to Honda bulletin 14-058, covered in Chapter 18, which once extended the steering system's warranty to 10 years or 150,000 miles <!-- F-WP08-012 -->. By now that warranty window has passed on any 2008 Fit, so a repair would come out of your own pocket <!-- F-WP08-012 -->. Still, knowing the car's history helps you judge its condition <!-- F-WP08-012 -->.

### Eight trouble spots owners report on first-generation Fits
These eight items come from real owner reports on first-generation Fit forums <!-- F-WP08-013 -->. They are not from Honda or the government, so they are labeled as owner reports rather than confirmed defects <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.

1. Owners report that motor mounts wear out over time, causing a clunk when shifting into or out of gear, or vibration at idle <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.
2. Owners report that the timing chain tensioner can develop a ticking or rattling noise under load, though a healthy engine on this car is naturally a little "ticky" too <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.
3. On manual-transmission cars, owners report that the clutch master or slave cylinder can suddenly feel spongy or stop working, sometimes with no visible fluid leak <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.
4. On automatic-transmission cars, owners report a shudder or hesitation holding a steady 55 to 65 miles per hour <!-- F-WP08-013 -->. More than one part can cause this, including worn spark plugs or ignition coils, not only the transmission <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.
5. Owners report air conditioning problems in two forms: a compressor that rattles when it engages, and cooling that starts strong but does not stay cold <!-- F-WP08-013 -->. Also check the condenser, which sits ahead of the radiator, for rock-strike damage <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.
6. Owners report a metallic clunk or knock from the front suspension over bumps, most often traced to worn sway-bar end links or strut top-mount bushings <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.
7. Owners report a dashboard or glovebox rattle, most often from a loose panel under the windshield or a part behind the factory radio <!-- F-WP08-013 -->. It tends to get worse in cold weather <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.
8. Owners report unexplained battery drain <!-- F-WP08-013 -->. Ask if the car has ever gone dead after sitting for a few days <!-- F-WP08-013 -->. A healthy 2008 Fit should draw roughly 15 to 17 **milliamps (mA)** of power with everything off <!-- F-WP08-013 -->. A milliamp (mA) is a small unit of electrical current <!-- F-WP08-013 -->.

Owners also report a specific rust spot worth checking, especially on a car that has spent winters in a state that salts its roads <!-- F-WP08-014 -->. Look where the rear quarter panel meets the rear bumper cover <!-- F-WP08-014 -->. Also check the seam where a plastic cover hides the joint between the quarter panel, the rocker panel, and the underside of the car <!-- F-WP08-014 -->. This pocket collects grit and moisture, and it is reported as the first place rust shows up on this car <!-- F-WP08-014 -->. Left unchecked, it can progress to holes in the driver's rear wheel well <!-- F-WP08-014 -->.

### One test drive, eight checks
You can check most of these trouble spots on a single test drive <!-- F-WP08-015 -->. Shift firmly into and out of gear and listen for a clunk, which points to worn motor mounts <!-- F-WP08-015 -->. Accelerate under moderate load and listen for a new ticking or rattling beyond the engine's normal light idle sound <!-- F-WP08-015 -->. On a manual car, push the clutch pedal fully and feel for sponginess or a change in pedal travel <!-- F-WP08-015 -->. On an automatic car, hold a steady 55 to 65 miles per hour and feel for shudder or hesitation <!-- F-WP08-015 -->. Run the air conditioning on max for several minutes and confirm it stays cold the whole time, not only for the first minute <!-- F-WP08-015 -->. Drive over a few bumps and rough pavement, listening for suspension clunks and dashboard rattles <!-- F-WP08-015 -->.

### What a used 2008 Fit costs in today's market
Different pricing sources measure different things, so their numbers do not match, and you should not read them as contradicting each other <!-- F-WP08-018 -->.

| Source | What it measures | Base-trim range |
|---|---|---|
| Kelley Blue Book (KBB) | Dealer retail price for the base "Hatchback 4D" trim | $4,575 to $5,850 <!-- F-WP08-016 --> |
| KBB | Estimated trade-in value (what a dealer might offer you for your own car) | About $2,550 to $2,825 <!-- F-WP08-016 --> |
| KBB | Estimated private-party value (selling car to car, not through a dealer) | About $3,925 to $5,125 <!-- F-WP08-016 --> |
| Edmunds | Appraisal-tool trade-in value | About $1,022 <!-- F-WP08-017 --> |
| Edmunds | Appraisal-tool private-party value | About $1,446 <!-- F-WP08-017 --> |
| CarGurus | Average price on cars actually listed for sale right now | About $6,521, with real listings ranging from roughly $2,600 to over $15,000 depending on the city <!-- F-WP08-017 --> |

Note: KBB's own figures for 2026 show the Sport trim pricing lower than the base "Hatchback 4D" trim <!-- F-WP08-016 -->. This book uses the Hatchback 4D numbers above for base-trim buying guidance, not the Sport numbers <!-- F-WP08-016 -->.

KBB and Edmunds are **appraisal-tool estimates**, meaning they assume an average, typical-mileage, typical-condition car, and both run lower than real asking prices <!-- F-WP08-018 -->. CarGurus reflects **actual asking prices** on real listings <!-- F-WP08-018 -->. That is why its number runs higher, and why it varies so much by city, mileage, and condition <!-- F-WP08-018 -->. This price gap between the two kinds of sources is a known, logged disagreement between them, not a mistake in one source or the other <!-- F-WP08-018 -->. A fair way to read all this: expect a rough floor around $1,000 to $3,000 for a high-mileage, rough-condition example <!-- F-WP08-018 -->. Expect a realistic asking price of roughly $3,000 to $10,000 or more for a clean, lower-mileage example <!-- F-WP08-018 -->. Use these numbers as a starting point, since used-car prices move over time and by region <!-- F-WP08-018 -->.

### What the odometer is likely to show
At the commonly cited average of about 12,000 miles driven per year, an 18-year-old car would show roughly 216,000 miles by simple math <!-- F-WP08-019 -->. The US Federal Highway Administration reports drivers actually average 13,400 to 14,300 miles per year <!-- F-WP08-019 -->. That would put the same math closer to 241,000 to 257,000 miles <!-- F-WP08-019 -->. Real 2008 Fit listings show much wider variation than either estimate. Two examples showed 206,169 miles and 128,493 miles <!-- F-WP08-019 -->. Many owners drive less than the national average, especially as a car gets older <!-- F-WP08-019 -->. Treat any reading from roughly 100,000 to 250,000 or more miles as plausible and normal for a car this age <!-- F-WP08-019 -->.

Routine maintenance, such as oil, filters, brakes, and tires, is covered elsewhere in this book <!-- F-WP08-020 -->. Beyond that, expect at least some of the eight trouble spots above to show wear, or to have already been fixed, on any real 100,000-plus-mile example <!-- F-WP08-020 -->. Also check the paint on horizontal surfaces like the roof and hood for clearcoat peeling, another item owners report on this car <!-- F-WP08-020 -->. Use what you find as a talking point for negotiating the price <!-- F-WP08-020 -->. Do not treat it as an automatic reason to walk away from an otherwise well cared for car <!-- F-WP08-020 -->.
