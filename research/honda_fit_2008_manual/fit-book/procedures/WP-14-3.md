Title: Replace the front struts

Skill level: 4 (shop recommended) — the front suspension uses a coil spring compressed around
the strut (a "MacPherson strut"), and a spring compressor tool that slips or is used incorrectly
can release enough stored energy to cause serious injury or death. Community writeups
consistently flag the spring-compression step, not the bolt-removal steps, as the real hazard
in this job.

Time: N/A for full DIY steps at this skill level — see "What the shop will do" below. An
experienced home mechanic with a quality spring compressor and hex-wrench set typically reports
60-90 minutes per side once practiced.

What the shop will do:
- Raise the car and remove the front wheel on each side.
- Disconnect the strut from the steering knuckle (2 bolts) and unbolt the brake hose bracket and any ABS sensor wiring clip from the strut, per the manufacturer's real assembly (Source: S-WP14-B, hfitinfo.com Front Damper/Spring pages, confirms this general disassembly shape).
- Remove the 3 top strut-mount nuts from inside the engine bay and lift the strut/spring assembly out.
- On the bench, compress the spring with a proper spring compressor, remove the top-mount nut while holding the shaft with a hex wrench, then disassemble and rebuild the strut with the new part, following the exact torque and reassembly order in the shop's service information (Source: S-WP14-B).
- Reinstall the new strut assembly in the reverse order and torque every fastener to spec.
- Perform a wheel alignment afterward — replacing struts changes ride height and can shift camber/toe slightly, so an alignment check is standard practice after this job.

Why this book does not give full DIY steps here: RESEARCH_PLAN.md's own torque-spec research
(facts/WP-10.md, F-WP10-008) could not confirm a strut top-nut torque value for this exact car
from two independent sources — only a single, unconfirmed community lead exists. Combined with
the spring-compression hazard, this book follows its own safety rule (D9: never skip a safety
step) by treating this as a shop-recommended job rather than guessing at a torque number or
downplaying the spring risk.

Safety warnings (read even if you plan to have a shop do this job, so you know what to ask about):
- Always assume a coil spring under a strut is storing enough energy to cause serious injury if it releases suddenly and uncontrolled.
- Do not attempt this job with an improvised spring compressor (rope, chain, or a bar clamp) — use a purpose-built strut spring compressor if you do this yourself.
- Always ask a shop to also check and, if needed, perform a wheel alignment after a strut replacement.

If you choose to do this yourself despite the above: use a full written procedure from a factory
or Haynes-style manual with the exact torque values for this car (Appendix C notes where those
values are UNVERIFIED for this generation), and do not skip the alignment check afterward.

How you know it worked: The car sits at an even, normal ride height on all four corners, there
is no clunking or knocking over bumps, the car does not pull to one side or bounce excessively
after hitting a bump, and a post-job alignment check comes back within spec.

Common mistakes:
- Skipping the alignment check after the job, leading to uneven tire wear.
- Reusing a worn or cracked top-mount bearing/bushing instead of replacing it along with the strut.
- Using a spring compressor that is not rated for this size of spring.
