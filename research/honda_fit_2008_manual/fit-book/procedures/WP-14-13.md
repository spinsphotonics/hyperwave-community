Chapter: 44 (Exhaust and O2 sensors)
Title: Replace an exhaust section or muffler

RESEARCH FINDING: the one real service-manual-style page found for this job on this car
(Source: S-WP14-A, hfitinfo.com "Exhaust Pipe and Muffler Replacement") extracted with almost no
readable step text — only a single reassembly note survived — so this book cannot show a
confirmed, this-car-specific, bolt-by-bolt sequence. The general steps below are standard
practice for a hanger-and-flange exhaust system on a small front-wheel-drive car, not a
Fit-specific script; treat this card as general technique, not a Honda-confirmed procedure.

Skill level: 3 (needs jack stands and care) — mainly because of rusted-fastener risk and working under a supported car, not because the mechanism is complex.

Time: 90-150 minutes for a first-timer, depending heavily on how rusted the old fasteners are

Tools:
- Jack and 2 jack stands (see P-WP11-22)
- Penetrating oil
- Wrench/socket set for the flange bolts or clamps at each joint
- A cutting tool (rotary tool with a cutting wheel, or a hacksaw), for a seized clamp or pipe that will not come apart cleanly
- A rubber mallet, to help separate rusted flange joints

Parts and supplies:
- New gaskets and self-locking nuts for any flange joint you open, since Honda's own note for this car specifically calls for new gaskets and self-locking nuts on reassembly, not reused ones [Source: S-WP14-A]
- New exhaust hangers (rubber mounts) for any that are cracked or torn
- The replacement pipe, muffler, or section itself — part number UNVERIFIED this pass; ask a Honda parts counter, a muffler shop, or check Appendix F

Safety warnings:
- Always let the exhaust system cool completely before touching it.
- Always wear eye protection, especially when using penetrating oil or a cutting tool on rusted fasteners.
- Do not work under a car held up only by a jack. Use jack stands.
- Do not cut near the fuel tank, fuel lines, or brake lines; know what is above and around any spot before you cut.
- Always support a heavy section (like the muffler) before loosening its last hanger, so it does not swing down and hit you or the ground hard.

Steps:
1. Make sure the exhaust system is completely cool before starting.
2. Raise the car and support it on jack stands.
3. Spray penetrating oil on every fastener and hanger rubber you plan to disturb, and let it sit.
4. Support the section you are removing (a jack, a strap, or a helper) before loosening its last fastener.
5. Separate the flange joint at each end of the section you are replacing, using a rubber mallet if the joint is rusted tight.
6. If a clamp or bolt is fully seized, cut it carefully rather than damaging the surrounding pipe by forcing it.
7. Remove the old section, working it free of its rubber hangers.
8. Compare the new section to the old one to confirm it matches before installing.
9. Fit new rubber hangers if the old ones are cracked or torn.
10. Position the new section and hang it loosely on its rubber mounts before tightening anything.
11. Fit new gaskets at each flange joint you opened.
12. Install new self-locking nuts at the flange joints [Source: S-WP14-A].
13. With the system hanging naturally (not twisted or under strain), tighten all flange fasteners.
14. Lower the car and start the engine, listening and looking for any exhaust leak or rattle.

How you know it worked: There is no hissing, ticking, or rattling sound from the exhaust with the engine idling, no visible exhaust leak at any joint, and the exhaust hangs evenly without touching the body or the ground when you look underneath.

Common mistakes:
- Reusing old gaskets or nuts at a flange joint instead of new ones, leading to a leak later.
- Tightening one flange fully before the whole section is loosely hung, which can twist and stress the joints.
- Cutting a stuck fastener without checking what is directly behind or above it first.
- Not supporting a heavy section before removing its last hanger.
