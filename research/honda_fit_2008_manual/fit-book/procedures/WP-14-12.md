Chapter: 44 (Exhaust and O2 sensors)
Title: Replace an oxygen (O2) sensor

RESEARCH FINDING: this car has two oxygen-type sensors in its exhaust path per cylinder bank
(this engine has one bank): a primary (upstream) air/fuel-ratio sensor threaded into the exhaust
manifold, and a secondary (downstream) oxygen sensor on the catalytic converter that watches the
converter's efficiency — this matches the OBD-II code table already researched in facts/WP-12.md
(P0135/P0141 for the primary sensor circuit, P0420 for catalyst efficiency, which depends on the
downstream sensor's reading) [Source: S-WP14-F]. Community sources describe the downstream
sensor's electrical connector as reached only after removing the center console on this
generation, which is more involved than the primary sensor's connector [Source: S-WP14-F].

Skill level: 2 (needs basic tools) for the primary/upstream sensor; treat the downstream sensor
as skill level 3 given the extra interior disassembly to reach its connector.

Time: 45 minutes for the primary sensor; 90 minutes for the downstream sensor including console removal

Tools:
- An oxygen-sensor socket (a socket with a slot for the sensor's wire) or a suitably-sized wrench
- Penetrating oil, for a sensor that has not been removed in years
- Anti-seize compound rated for oxygen sensors (many new sensors come pre-coated; do not add extra to the sensor's tip)
- Jack and 2 jack stands (see P-WP11-22), if working from underneath
- Basic interior trim tools, only if replacing the downstream sensor and removing the center console

Parts and supplies:
- New oxygen/air-fuel-ratio sensor matched to the correct position (upstream vs. downstream) for this car — part number UNVERIFIED this pass; ask a Honda parts counter or check Appendix F, and confirm upstream vs. downstream before ordering, since they are different parts

Safety warnings:
- Always let the exhaust system cool completely before touching it; it stays dangerously hot long after the engine is off.
- Always wear eye protection, especially when using penetrating oil on a rusted sensor.
- Do not work under a car held up only by a jack. Use jack stands.
- Do not overtighten a new sensor; overtightening can crack the sensor body.

Steps:
1. Make sure the exhaust system is completely cool before starting.
2. Identify whether you are replacing the upstream (primary, on the exhaust manifold) or downstream (secondary, on the catalytic converter) sensor.
3. Raise the car and support it on jack stands if working from underneath.
4. Disconnect the sensor's electrical connector — for the downstream sensor, this may require removing the center console first [Source: S-WP14-F].
5. Apply penetrating oil to the sensor's threads if it looks rusted, and let it sit a few minutes.
6. Use the oxygen-sensor socket or wrench to loosen and remove the old sensor.
7. Compare the new sensor's connector and thread to the old one to confirm a correct match.
8. Thread the new sensor in by hand first to avoid cross-threading.
9. Tighten it with the socket or wrench to a snug fit; do not overtighten.
10. Reconnect the sensor's electrical connector.
11. Reinstall the center console if you removed it for the downstream sensor.
12. Lower the car if raised.
13. Clear any stored check-engine codes related to the sensor, if your scan tool supports it, and take a test drive.

How you know it worked: The check-engine light does not return for the sensor code you were chasing after a normal test drive, and a scan tool (if you have one) shows the sensor reading changing normally as the engine runs, rather than staying flat.

Common mistakes:
- Confusing the upstream and downstream sensor and ordering the wrong part.
- Overtightening the new sensor and cracking its body.
- Forcing a rusted sensor without penetrating oil, snapping it off in the exhaust manifold or converter.
- Not clearing the old fault code, leaving the check-engine light on even after a correct repair until enough clean drive cycles pass.
