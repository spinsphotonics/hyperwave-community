## Section A: Documents that come with a car in the US (Chapter 17) — kept generic

Fact ID: F-WP08-001
Chapter: 17
Question: What documents come with a car bought from a dealer in the US (general)?
Value: When buying from a licensed dealer, you should receive: (1) the vehicle's title or
Manufacturer's Certificate of Origin/proof the dealer can transfer title, (2) a completed Bill
of Sale / Retail Certificate of Sale (the dealer's own sales contract, showing price, taxes,
and fees), (3) a Buyers Guide window sticker (federally required on used cars — see
F-WP08-004) that becomes the buyer's copy at sale, (4) an odometer disclosure statement (the
mileage reading, signed by the seller, required by federal law for most vehicles under 20 model
years old at time of sale), and (5) a temporary registration/license plate or a completed
application to title and register the car, so the buyer can drive it legally until permanent
plates arrive. Exact paperwork names and processes vary by state — check your state DMV site.

Fact ID: F-WP08-002

Question: What documents come with a car bought from a private party in the US (general)?
Value: In a private-party sale you should receive: (1) the title, signed over to you by the
seller (whose name must match the name already on the title, or a chain of title/power of

DMV so the seller is not held responsible for the car after the sale date. Exact forms and

Fact ID: F-WP08-004
Chapter: 17
Question: What is the federal "Buyers Guide" window sticker, and what must it disclose?
Value: The FTC's Used Car Rule requires dealers (not private sellers) to post a "Buyers Guide"
sticker in the window of every used car offered for sale, before the car is displayed or a
customer is allowed to inspect it for buying purposes. The Buyers Guide must disclose: whether
the dealer offers any warranty and, if so, its length, what percentage of repair costs the
dealer will pay, and which of the vehicle's systems it covers; a list of the vehicle's 14 major
systems and the kinds of defects that can occur in them; a suggestion to ask the dealer if a
pre-purchase inspection by an independent mechanic is allowed; a warning not to rely on spoken
promises not put in writing; and (since a 2018 revision) a recommendation to get a vehicle
history report, pointing to ftc.gov/usedcars. The dealer must give the buyer this same sticker
as their copy at the time of sale.

## Section B: Documents to keep in the glovebox and at home (Chapter 19) — kept generic

Fact ID: F-WP08-005
Chapter: 19
Question: What documents should you keep in the glovebox?
Value: Keep in the glovebox: current vehicle registration, proof of insurance card, and (if
your state issues one) a current safety/emissions inspection sticker or certificate. Some
owners also keep a copy of the FTC Buyers Guide sticker (see F-WP08-004) and a card listing an
emergency contact and any known medical conditions or allergies. Do NOT keep the title in the
glovebox — a stolen title makes a stolen car much easier to resell (see F-WP08-006).

## Section C: How to check a recall by VIN (Chapters 18/20)

Fact ID: F-WP08-007
Chapter: 18
Question: What is NHTSA's actual VIN-based recall lookup tool, and how do you use it?
Value: NHTSA's official recall-check tool is at **nhtsa.gov/recalls**. The page has a
"Search by YMM or VIN" option with a 17-character "VIN Lookup" field (the page notes: "The VIN
is protected under the Privacy Act" and "Providing your VIN and/or license plate number is
voluntary"). Typing in the car's full 17-character VIN and clicking Search returns whether that
exact vehicle has any open, unrepaired safety recall. The VIN is found on a small plate at the
lower left corner of the windshield (visible from outside the car), and is also printed on the
vehicle's registration card and often the insurance card. A search by license plate + state is
also offered as an alternative to VIN.

Fact ID: F-WP08-008

Question: What does the VIN recall search result actually tell you?
Value: Searching by VIN or license plate tells you specifically whether THAT vehicle still has
an open, unrepaired recall — if it has none, the tool shows the message "0 unrepaired recalls
associated with this VIN." Searching instead by year/make/model (with no VIN) gives general

model, not a per-vehicle repaired/unrepaired status. The page's own guidance notes the
license-plate/VIN search only shows unrepaired-recall status "for certain manufacturers" —
implying coverage/participation can vary — and does not necessarily show every kind of
recall-adjacent record (e.g., some complaint or investigation data may need the general
year/make/model search instead).

## Section D: Pre-purchase inspection — general checklist (Chapter 20)

Fact ID: F-WP08-010
Chapter: 20
Question: What does a general pre-purchase inspection cover, regardless of make/model?
Value: A general pre-purchase inspection (PPI), whether done by the buyer or a paid independent
mechanic, typically covers five areas: (1) Exterior/body — crash damage, mismatched paint
(a sign of prior repair), rust, flood-damage signs, whether the car sits level, and cracked or
chipped glass; (2) Tires and brakes — tread depth, tire age (from the sidewall date code),
uneven wear, and pad/rotor/caliper condition; (3) Engine bay and undercarriage — fluid leaks
(a puddle under a parked car is worth investigating; transmission leaks are especially
expensive), belt and hose condition, and frame/subframe rust; (4) Interior and electronics —
HVAC function, instrument cluster and warning lights, seat condition and adjustment, and that
every gauge, light, and indicator actually works; (5) a test drive checking braking, steering
feel, suspension behavior, and acceleration, plus (6) a scan for stored diagnostic trouble
codes (DTCs) in the car's computer, since a code can be cleared right before a sale but the car
driven long enough afterward to reset it. A professional PPI from an independent shop
(recommended by the FTC's own Buyers Guide sticker, see F-WP08-004) commonly costs about
$100-$200.

Question: Which TSB-related item is worth asking about specifically before buying?
Value: Ask whether the car has ever thrown a Malfunction Indicator Lamp (check engine light)
with a steering-related trouble code, and whether the electric power steering (EPS) has ever
felt unusually heavy or hard to turn, especially when not moving. Honda's own service bulletin

models) with stored diagnostic code 32-09 or 61-04 — by 2026 a typical 2008 Fit is past both of
those limits, so this repair would now be an out-of-pocket cost, not a warranty claim, but
knowing it happened (or that the EPS still feels normal) is still useful buying information.

Fact ID: F-WP08-013
Chapter: 20
Question: What community-reported mechanical wear items should a buyer specifically check on a
used 2008 Fit, based on known first-generation trouble spots?
Value: Owners report (community-reported, Tier 4, see facts/WP-02.md Section F for full
sourcing on each item) that these areas are worth checking specifically on a first-generation
(GD3) Fit: (1) Motor mounts — listen/feel for a clunk when shifting into or out of gear, or
under abrupt acceleration/braking, and for vibration at idle (F-WP02-016); (2) Timing/cam chain
tensioner — listen for a ticking or rattling noise under engine load that goes away at idle or
coasting, keeping in mind owners describe even a healthy L15A1 engine as naturally "ticky"
(F-WP02-017); (3) Clutch master/slave cylinder, MANUAL transmission only — check for a suddenly
spongy or unresponsive clutch pedal, sometimes with no visible external fluid leak
(F-WP02-018); (4) Automatic transmission shudder, AUTOMATIC transmission only — during the test
drive, hold a steady 55-65 mph on flat ground and feel for a shudder or hesitation (owners note
this symptom has more than one possible cause, including ignition coils or spark plugs, not
only the transmission itself) (F-WP02-019); (5) A/C — check that the compressor engages
smoothly without rattling and that cooling stays consistent, not just cold for a minute after
startup, and look at the condenser (ahead of the radiator) for visible rock-strike damage
(F-WP02-020); (6) Front suspension clunk — a metallic clunk or knock over bumps, most often
traced to worn sway-bar end links or strut top-mount bushings (F-WP02-021); (7) Dashboard/
glovebox rattle — a common owner complaint, most often a loose cowl panel under the windshield
or a component behind the factory radio, worse in cold weather (F-WP02-022); (8) Battery drain
— ask if the car has ever gone dead after a few days of non-use; on a healthy 2008 Fit,
parasitic draw with everything off should be roughly 15-17 mA (F-WP02-025).

Fact ID: F-WP08-014
Chapter: 20
Question: What should a buyer specifically check for body rust on a used 2008 Fit?
Value: Owners report (community-reported, see facts/WP-02.md F-WP02-024) a specific,
frequently-cited rust location on first-generation Fits: where the rear quarter panel meets
the rear bumper cover, plus the seam where a plastic cover conceals the join of the rear
quarter panel, rocker panel, and undercarriage — this pocket collects road grit and moisture
and is reported as the first place rust appears. In more neglected cases this can progress to
holes in the driver's rear wheel well. This is reported as much more of a concern for cars
driven in road-salt climates, and is largely preventable if caught and treated early. A buyer
in (or shopping a car titled out of) a salt-belt state should look here specifically, in
addition to the usual rocker-panel and wheel-arch rust checks any used car needs.
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (community-reported; salt/climate-dependent)

average, especially in a car's later years, so a wide range of odometer readings (very roughly
