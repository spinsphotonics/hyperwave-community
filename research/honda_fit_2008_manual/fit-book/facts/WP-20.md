Fact ID: F-WP20-003
Chapter: 58
Question: What general category of modifications would move a stock 2008 Fit from H Street (HS)
up to SCCA's Street Touring category, and is the Fit classed there too?
Value: Yes — "Fit" is also listed (again with no model-year qualifier) in the rulebook's E Street
Touring (EST) class, inside a shared "catch-all" list of Honda small cars (Accord 4-cyl, several
Civic ranges, CRX, CR-Z, del Sol, Fit, Insight). Street Touring is the rulebook's own
next-step-up category, described as sitting "between the current Street and Street Prepared
categories." Per the rulebook's Section 14 category description, moving a car into Street Touring
means adding the kinds of parts Street category alone does not allow: aftermarket suspension
(shocks/springs/sway bars), a different (limited-slip or aftermarket) differential, bolt-on engine
parts, a larger or aftermarket brake kit, and wider or different-sized wheels/tires than the
Street-category tire rule allows — while the rulebook still expects the car to stay legal to
drive on public roads and keep its emissions equipment, OBD-II system, and factory ABS
functioning (ABS may only be electronically disabled, not removed). In short: a plain fluids/
tires-only Fit stays in HS; the same car with a Street-Touring-legal suspension, brake, and wheel/
tire package (and no more than that) would run in EST instead.

## Section B: NASA/HPDE tech inspection (Chapter 59)

Fact ID: F-WP20-005
Chapter: 59
Question: What does a typical HPDE tech inspection actually check on the car itself (the
mechanical/chassis items), per a real published organization's rules?
Value: Per NASA's own Club Codes and Regulations (CCR), Section 11.4 "Vehicle Technical
Regulations" (which governs NASA HPDE, not just competition racing), the mechanical items checked
before a car goes on track include: **Wheels and tires** — no cracked or damaged wheels, no
exposed cords or bubbles on the tire, every lug nut present and tight. **Steering and suspension**
— front and rear wheel bearings checked for tightness/play, and very little or no play allowed in
the suspension or steering. **Engine bay** — no significant fluid leaks from the engine, radiator,
or hoses; a radiator overflow container of at least one liter; any oil breather/vent either
returned to the engine or routed to a catch tank of at least one liter; hoses in good condition
with no cracks. **Brakes** — good working condition able to stop the car in a reasonable, safe
distance; adequate pedal pressure; fluid at or above the manufacturer's minimum level; brake lines
in good condition. **General appearance** — the car must be undamaged-looking and straight enough
to pass what the rulebook calls the "50/50 rule" (look undamaged and straight at 50 mph from 50
feet).

Fact ID: F-WP20-006
Chapter: 59
Question: What safety-equipment and general-condition items does a typical HPDE tech inspection
check, beyond the car's mechanicals, and how does the tech-inspection process itself work?
Value: The same NASA CCR section also covers non-mechanical safety items: **Seatbelts and
harnesses** — factory belts must be in good, undamaged, factory configuration (or an approved
aftermarket harness for cars using aftermarket hard-shell seats). **Roll bars** — required on all
open (convertible/roadster) cars, with padding where required; not required on a closed car like a
Fit for HPDE. **Battery** — must be securely fastened; a bungee or rubber cord alone is not an
acceptable hold-down; the positive terminal must be covered with a non-conductive material.
**Fuel caps** — must seal well enough that fuel will not spill under hard driving. **Exposed
wires** — no exposed live wiring inside the cabin. **Seats** — securely fastened to the car's
structure. **Loose objects** — everything loose must be removed from the passenger compartment
before going on track. **Mirrors** — at least one working rear-view mirror. **Brake lights** — at
least two working, visible from 300 feet. **Tow eyes** — strongly recommended, one front and one
rear, so a stranded car can be safely towed off track. On process: per the CCR's Section 11.2, a
driver is expected to review the Technical Inspection Form before the event, either have the car
pre-teched at an authorized shop or teched at the track itself, and once the form is signed, place
it on the dash or windshield for a grid marshal to collect and apply a tech sticker.

## Section C: Event-day checklist (Chapter 60)

Fact ID: F-WP20-008
Chapter: 60
Question: What should a driver do the night before a track day / HPDE event?
Value: General good-practice advice, synthesized from a reputable track-day-focused industry
source (Turn 14 Distribution, a major performance-parts distributor) and a manufacturer's own
track-day guide (Hyundai N): finish any car prep several days ahead rather than the night before —
last-minute repairs that go wrong (a broken bolt, an unexpected issue) are one of the most common
ways a driver arrives at the track exhausted and unprepared. The night before, pack the car and
gear bag completely so the morning is simple: helmet and gloves, the completed (or shop-signed)
tech inspection form and a pen, a tire pressure gauge, a small tool bag with a jack, wheel-lock key
if applicable, and zip ties, a filled gas container if the venue does not sell fuel, a cooler
packed with water/sports drink and food, cash (for tow-truck tips or tolls), and a toll transponder
if driving toll roads to the track. Many event organizers also hold check-in the evening before, so
attending it avoids a morning-of line for waivers, the tech form, car numbers, and the event
wristband/tech sticker.

Fact ID: F-WP20-010
Chapter: 60
Question: What should a driver check or do between track sessions, during the event day itself?
Value: Recheck tire pressure and, if a temperature gun is available, tire temperature after each
session — track surface temperature and driving intensity both change pressure through the day,
and a reputable industry checklist source treats a tire pressure gauge as one of the handful of
"cannot forget" tools for exactly this reason. Recheck wheel/lug torque periodically, since track
driving can work fasteners loose faster than street driving. Top off engine oil if the car is a
known consumer (or simply check the level) using oil and a funnel packed for this purpose. Use the
break between sessions to rehydrate, eat something, and rest in the shade rather than only working
on the car — staying hydrated and fed is repeatedly called out as directly affecting how well and
how safely a driver performs later in the day. Many organized HPDE programs also hold an
instructor debrief or driver meeting between sessions; taking a few notes on what changed
(braking points, an odd noise, a new tire pressure) between sessions is a widely repeated
practical habit for tracking progress and problems across the day.

Fact ID: F-WP20-012
Chapter: 61
Question: What do reputable driving instructors teach as the fundamental of smooth steering and
throttle inputs, and why does smoothness matter?
Value: The same source teaches holding the steering wheel at the 9-and-3 o'clock position for
almost every corner on almost every track, and treats minimizing how much the hands move on the
wheel as directly tied to how smooth — and ultimately how fast — a driver can be: less wheel
movement means a smoother, more predictable car. The underlying reason given is weight transfer:
braking shifts weight (and traction) toward the front tires, accelerating shifts it toward the
rear, and turning shifts it toward the outside tires, and every one of those inputs, done abruptly,
causes more weight transfer than the same input done gradually. Because a tire's grip does not
increase in a straight-line proportion to the extra load it receives, a car loses more overall
grip from an abrupt input than a smooth one — which the source summarizes as the reason "smooth is
fast," not just safer.

Fact ID: F-WP20-013
Chapter: 61
Question: What is trail braking, in basic terms a beginner can understand, per a reputable
driving-instruction source?
Value: Trail braking is described as gradually easing (trailing) the foot off the brake pedal
while turning into a corner, rather than releasing the brake completely before starting to turn.
Doing this trades some of the car's remaining braking force for cornering force as the corner
develops, which the source explains helps keep the front tires closer to their maximum traction
through turn-in (since braking keeps extra weight, and therefore extra grip, on the front tires
right as the driver is asking them to also change the car's direction). The same material pairs
this idea with a related beginner-level concept: many faster drivers focus less on exactly where
they start braking and more on precisely where they finish releasing the brake pedal, because a
smoother, later brake release both balances the car better and forces the driver to be looking
into the corner rather than down at the pedal.

## Section E: Post-event inspection (Chapter 62)

Fact ID: F-WP20-014
Chapter: 62
Question: What should a driver do to the car immediately after finishing on-track sessions, before
driving it home?
Value: A manufacturer-published track-day guide (Hyundai N) recommends taking a cool-down lap
(driving one slower, gentle lap) at the end of a session rather than braking hard and parking
immediately, to let air move through the brakes, engine, and transmission and bring temperatures
down gradually; the same source specifically warns against using the parking/hand brake right
after a hot session, since a very hot brake rotor clamped by the parking brake can distort or
damage the pad/rotor as it cools. Once the car and tires have cooled, recheck tire pressure and
tread condition before the drive home — hot-session pressures run higher than the correct
driving pressure, and a session can reveal cuts, cords, or uneven wear that were not there in the
morning. The same source and a track-day-focused industry checklist both also call for a basic
brake check at this point: the pedal should still feel firm, not soft or noticeably longer in
travel than it did that morning, since brake fluid can vaporize or fade under sustained hard track
use.

Fact ID: F-WP20-015
Chapter: 62
Question: Once home, what should a driver check on the car after a track day — the fuller
post-event inspection?
Value: Look under the car and around the engine bay on clean, dry ground for any fresh drips of
oil, coolant, or brake fluid that were not there before the event, and verify all of the same fluid
levels checked before the event (engine oil, coolant, brake fluid) are still correct — sustained
high-RPM and hard-braking use consumes and stresses fluids faster than ordinary street driving.
Check visible brake pad thickness at each wheel and look for fresh cracks, heavy discoloration, or
fluid residue near the calipers, since pad wear is one of the fastest-consumed items on a track
day. Inspect all four tires for uneven wear, cuts, or a change in tread surface texture, and
re-torque every wheel's lug nuts once the car and wheels are fully cool, since track driving's
repeated heating-and-cooling cycles and higher cornering loads can work fasteners loose faster
than normal driving. Finally, do a general walk-around/listen check for anything that came loose
in the engine bay, under the car, or in the interior (trim panels, undertray fasteners, exhaust
hangers) before putting the car back into everyday use.

- **Chapter 58 (Autocross):** A stock 2008 base Fit currently runs in **H Street (HS)** under
  SCCA's National Solo rules — confirmed in both the 2026 (Feb 2026) and 2025 (March 2025
  Fastrack) rulebook editions, with identical, unqualified-by-year "Fit" listings in all three
  relevant classes. Adding Street-Touring-legal suspension/brake/wheel-tire mods moves it to

  exhaust moves it to **F Street Prepared (FSP)**. A 2008 model-year car remains eligible for SCCA
  National events through 2038 under the rulebook's 30-year rule (F-WP20-002), so eligibility is
  not a near-term concern.
- **Chapter 59 (HPDE tech inspection):** NASA's own published Club Codes and Regulations (Section
  11.4) and a real fillable NASA tech form both check the same core items: tire/wheel condition
  and lug nuts, steering/suspension play, engine-bay leaks, brake condition/pedal feel/fluid,
  battery security and terminal cover, seatbelt condition, no loose objects, and a securely closing
  fuel cap. Worn brake pads, a loose battery, and cracked/corded tires are the most commonly
  reported first-timer tech failures (F-WP20-007).
- **Chapter 60 (Event-day checklist):** Night-before prep and check-in, cold-tire-pressure and

  three recurring phases across the reputable sources used (F-WP20-008 through F-WP20-010).

  levels, brake pad thickness, tire wear, and re-torquing lug nuts (F-WP20-015).
