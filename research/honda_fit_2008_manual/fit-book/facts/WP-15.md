## Section A — Federal emissions tampering rules (EPA, Chapter 47)

Fact ID: F-WP15-001
Chapter: 47
Question: What does the Clean Air Act say about manufacturing, selling, or installing aftermarket parts that defeat emissions controls?
Value: The Clean Air Act makes it illegal for "any person to manufacture or sell, or offer to sell, or install, any part or component intended for use with, or as part of, any motor vehicle or motor vehicle engine, where a principal effect of the part or component is to bypass, defeat, or render inoperative any device or element of design installed on or in a motor vehicle or motor vehicle engine in compliance with [Clean Air Act Title II] regulations, and where the person knows or should know that such part or component is being offered for sale or installed for such use or put to such use" (CAA § 203(a)(3)(B), 42 U.S.C. § 7522(a)(3)(B)). This is the "defeat device" rule — it targets whoever makes, sells, or installs the part.
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (yes) — this is a general federal rule that applies to every EPA-certified motor vehicle in the US, the 2008 Fit included; it names no specific make or model.

Fact ID: F-WP15-002
Chapter: 47
Question: Is it a separate violation to remove or disable emissions equipment yourself, after you already own the car?
Value: Yes. The Clean Air Act has a second, "companion" anti-tampering provision that separately makes it illegal for any person to "knowingly remove or render inoperative any device or element installed on or in a motor vehicle or motor vehicle engine" after the vehicle has been sold to the consumer (CAA § 203(a)(3)(A), 42 U.S.C. § 7522(a)(3)(A)). This is the rule that applies directly to an owner doing the work themselves, separate from the rule against selling or installing the defeat-device part in F-WP15-001.
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (yes) — general federal rule, applies to any owner of any EPA-certified vehicle.

Fact ID: F-WP15-003
Chapter: 47
Question: Does EPA go after every owner who installs an aftermarket intake, header, or exhaust? What is the actual enforcement line?
Value: EPA states it uses "enforcement discretion" and is concerned specifically with aftermarket parts "that increase emissions." EPA generally takes no enforcement action against the sale or use of an aftermarket part if the person can show a "reasonable basis" for believing the part will not hurt emissions performance, proven one of three ways: (1) the part is identical in design and function to the original part it replaces; (2) the modified vehicle still meets emissions standards on the same tests the manufacturer used to certify it; or (3) the part has a CARB Executive Order (EO) covering that exact device on that exact vehicle (see Section B below).
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (yes) — general federal enforcement policy, applies the same way to any owner's aftermarket parts.

Fact ID: F-WP15-004
Chapter: 47
Question: What are the actual dollar penalties for violating the Clean Air Act's tampering/defeat-device rules?
Value: As of January 13, 2020 (the date stated in EPA's own fact sheet), the statutory civil penalty is up to $48,192 per violating vehicle, engine, or defeat device for a manufacturer or dealer, and up to $4,819 per violating vehicle, engine, or defeat device for any other person (CAA § 205, 42 U.S.C. § 7524; 40 C.F.R. § 19.4). EPA's fact sheet also states that knowingly falsifying, tampering with, or failing to install a required "monitoring device or method" (e.g., emissions-related onboard diagnostics) is a separate criminal violation (CAA § 113(c)(2)(C)).

Fact ID: F-WP15-006
Chapter: 47
Question: Is the federal tampering rule only a California thing, or does it apply everywhere?
Value: The Clean Air Act's tampering and defeat-device prohibitions (F-WP15-001, F-WP15-002) are federal law and apply in all 50 states, not just California — EPA's fact sheet frames them as protecting "the Nation's air resources." CARB (California's own air regulator) is a separate, additional layer: its Executive Order program is a California-specific certification system that EPA recognizes as one accepted way (of three) to prove the federal "reasonable basis" test in F-WP15-003. A part can therefore be both a federal Clean Air Act question (does it increase emissions?) and, for a car titled or registered in California or certain other states that have adopted California's vehicle-emissions rules, also a state smog-check/CARB-EO question layered on top. This book's own scope (D1) is a US-market Fit, and state emissions/inspection rules are called out elsewhere in the outline as "check your state" (Chapter 17); this fact card only establishes that the federal rule itself is nationwide.
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (yes) — general jurisdictional scope statement.

## Section B — CARB Executive Order (EO) exemption numbers (Chapter 47)

Fact ID: F-WP15-007
Chapter: 47
Question: What is a CARB Executive Order (EO) exemption number, in plain terms?
Value: A CARB Executive Order (EO) is a certificate the California Air Resources Board issues for a specific aftermarket or add-on part after CARB's own engineering evaluation shows the part does not increase vehicle emissions. Getting an EO grants that part an exemption from California's anti-tampering law for the specific vehicle(s) it is approved for. Every approved EO part has an assigned EO number, which a smog-check station, a BAR (Bureau of Automotive Repair) referee station, or CARB itself can look up and verify.
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (yes) — general definition; applies to any part a reader might install on this car.

Fact ID: F-WP15-008
Chapter: 47
Question: Does one EO number cover a part for every car it might physically bolt onto, or is it more specific than that?
Value: No — an Executive Order is tied to a specific engine family and evaporative-emissions family, and is valid only for the model year(s) named on it. CARB's own page states: "An Executive Order is valid for production during the specified model year. Vehicles produced for another model year require another Executive Order." In practice this means a reader must confirm an EO explicitly lists the 2008 Fit (or the correct engine family covering the L15A1) — an EO written for a different model year, or for the second-generation 2009-2014 Fit (GE8), does not automatically cover a 2007-2008 GD3, per this project's own D8 year-confusion rule.
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (yes, with the caution stated above) — this is exactly the kind of year-confusion risk D8 requires flagging.

## Section C — Typical insurance implications of modifying a car (Chapter 47)

Fact ID: F-WP15-012
Chapter: 47
Question: Does a normal car insurance policy automatically cover aftermarket parts?
Value: Generally, no. A standard auto policy is built around the car's factory configuration. Progressive's own consumer-education page states that "any vehicle enhancement not made at the factory is considered a modification and requires custom vehicle insurance" — covering both performance modifications (e.g., suspension changes, strut bars, turbochargers) and cosmetic ones (custom paint, stereo systems, special lighting). To be reimbursed for the full value of those parts, an owner typically needs an added policy endorsement (Progressive calls its version "custom parts and equipment coverage") or a separate custom-car policy, and this only works on top of comprehensive and collision coverage.
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (yes) — general insurance-industry practice, not car-specific.

Fact ID: F-WP15-014
Chapter: 47
Question: Do all modifications raise insurance cost, or can some lower it?
Value: It depends on the type of modification. Performance and appearance modifications (bigger engine output, custom paint, expensive aftermarket parts) generally raise cost, both because they raise the car's replacement value and because aftermarket parts often cost more to repair or replace than factory parts. Safety and security modifications (alarms, anti-theft systems, backup cameras and parking sensors, adaptive headlights) can qualify for a discount — but the source cautions this is not guaranteed, since some of that added technology is itself expensive to repair and can push the rate back up instead.
Applies to: 2008 GD3 base trim (yes) — general insurance-industry practice.
