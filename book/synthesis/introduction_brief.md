# Introduction Brief

Ten contrasts and continuities visible once the comparative tables are built, for a human editor
to write the book's Introduction from. This is evidence, not prose for the Introduction itself:
each bullet names the specific table row(s) it rests on so the editor can go check the underlying
dossier citation directly.

---

1. **Doctrinal subscription without personal testimony, and personal testimony without doctrinal
   subscription, are both "normal" — just three centuries apart.** Geneva Academy (1559) required
   students to sign a profession of faith but explicitly had no narrated-conversion requirement;
   Basel Mission (1815) made "a convincing account of spiritual rebirth," confirmed by a witness,
   the single decisive admission criterion; London Theological Seminary (1977) required a
   candidate to "be a converted man...aware of new life in himself." The book's oldest and
   youngest institutions sit at opposite ends of this axis, with the mid-century faith missions in
   between. — *Table 1, "Conversion/testimony required" column*: Geneva Academy, Basel Mission,
   China Inland Mission, Pastors' College, London Theological Seminary rows.

2. **Fee schedules ran from a graduated aristocratic tariff to an absolute, stated refusal to turn
   away the poor.** Scotland's First Book of Discipline (1560) proposed a university entrance fee
   scaled precisely by the father's social rank — an earl's son paying forty shillings, a
   husbandman's or burgess's son ten. Three hundred years later the Pastors' College (1856) stated
   the opposite as a point of principle: "if he cannot contribute a sixpence, he is equally
   welcome, and is received upon the same footing in all respects." — *Table 4, "Fees charged"
   column*: Scotland: First Book of Discipline, Pastors' College rows (compare also China Inland
   Mission and Andover Theological Seminary's Associate Foundation, both explicitly fee-free).

3. **The length of "the course" collapsed from years to months as the book moves from academies
   to Bible institutes.** Princeton Theological Seminary (1812) fixed a minimum of three years;
   Andover (1808) likewise fixed three; the College of New Jersey (1746) ran a fixed four-year
   sequence of named classes. By contrast the Missionary Training Institute at Nyack (1882) began
   as a single year (extended to three only in 1885), and Moody Bible Institute (1886) settled on
   two — while the China Inland Mission (1865) trained its "Probationers" mostly in the field,
   with no institutional course at all. — *Table 2, "Length of course" column*: Princeton
   Theological Seminary, Andover Theological Seminary, College of New Jersey rows versus
   Missionary Training Institute Nyack, Moody Bible Institute, China Inland Mission rows.

4. **Doctrinal precision and doctrinal minimalism are both explicit, stated policies — never an
   accident of omission.** Andover's Associate Creed names nine specific heresies its professors
   swear opposition to by name (Jews, "Mahometans," Arians, Pelagians, Antinomians, Arminians,
   Socinians, Unitarians, Universalists). The London Missionary Society (1795), founded thirteen
   years later, states with equal explicitness that "our design is not to send Presbyterianism,
   Independency, Episcopacy, or any other form of Church Order and Government" — deliberate
   silence as its own doctrinal position. — *Table 6*: Andover Theological Seminary row versus
   London Missionary Society and Church Missionary Society rows.

5. **Women were admitted as full missionaries roughly a century before any dossier records a
   woman admitted as a seminary student — and the book's very last founded institution reverses
   the trend outright.** The China Inland Mission (1865) sent nine women in its first major party
   and made "unmarried Candidates of either sex" explicit in its own rule-document; the East
   London Institute (1873) opened a separate Female Branch by 1884-85; the Student Volunteer
   Movement counted women among its own officers from 1888. London Theological Seminary, founded
   in 1977 — the last institution in this book — states flatly: "no women will be admitted." —
   *Table 1, "Women admitted" column*: China Inland Mission, East London Institute, Missionary
   Training Institute Nyack, Student Volunteer Movement rows versus London Theological Seminary
   row.

6. **A classical-language entrance examination was the norm for two centuries, then became the
   thing several institutions defined themselves against.** Harvard (1636), the College of New
   Jersey (1746), Andover (1808), and Princeton Seminary (1812) all required Latin and Greek
   proficiency, tested at the door. The Pastors' College (1856) states the reversal in its own
   words: "even brethren who could not read have been able to enter, and have been among the most
   useful of our students." — *Table 1, "Prior education required" column*: Harvard College,
   College of New Jersey, Andover Theological Seminary, Princeton Theological Seminary rows versus
   Pastors' College, East London Institute, China Inland Mission rows.

7. **The "faith principle" of financial support — no fee, no guaranteed salary, no solicitation,
   no debt — does not appear in any of the book's Reformation or Puritan academies, all of which
   ran on fixed stipends and endowments, and then becomes the named, explicit financial doctrine
   of nearly every faith mission from 1865 onward.** Geneva's chairs carried a fixed 280-florin
   salary; Emmanuel College fixed a weekly stipend to the shilling; Harvard was funded by a
   colonial grant and private bequests. The China Inland Mission's own rule states the reversal as
   a matter of theological principle, not merely practicality: "no personal solicitations or
   collections being authorized...going into debt being considered inconsistent with the
   principle of entire dependence upon God." — *Table 4, "Salary or 'faith' principle" column*:
   Geneva Academy, Emmanuel College Cambridge, Harvard College rows versus China Inland Mission,
   East London Institute, Missionary Training Institute Nyack, Prairie Bible Institute, CMML/
   Brethren rows.

8. **Missionary mortality was catastrophic in the 1730s and comparatively rare, then suddenly
   catastrophic again, in the China Inland Mission's history.** Of twenty-nine Moravian Brethren
   sent to St Croix in 1734-35, only three survived; a hundred and sixty Herrnhut missionaries died
   in St Thomas alone in the first fifty years. By contrast, the China Inland Mission recorded no
   member killed by violence or accident for thirty-three years (1865-1898) — until the 1900 Boxer
   crisis killed fifty-two CIM adults and sixteen children within a single year. — *Table 5,
   "Recorded deaths in service" column*: Herrnhut/Moravian Missions row versus China Inland Mission
   row.

9. **A pattern repeats across three centuries: an informal, single-teacher, no-diploma
   institution is judged insufficient by its own alumni's generation and answered by a more
   elaborate, formally organized successor.** The Log College's own founders trained ministers
   under one roof with no charter and no diploma; the College of New Jersey's own founders judged
   this "comparatively meagre" and built something of a deliberately "higher order" in direct
   response. A century and a half later the same shape recurs among the Bible institutes: the East
   London Institute's single classroom grew, within twenty years, into Nyack's and then Moody's
   named, multi-department curricula (Biblical, Literary, Theological, Practical, Missionary). —
   *Table 2, "Length of course" and structural detail*: Log College and College of New Jersey rows;
   see also `lineage_chart.md`, the Log College → College of New Jersey and East London Institute
   → Nyack → Moody Bible Institute edges.

10. **The word "sending" names two entirely different institutional mechanisms, a few centuries
    apart, and both are called by the same word.** Emmanuel College Cambridge (1584) "sent"
    graduates by presenting its own men to specific parish livings already in the College's own
    gift — a closed, single-institution pipeline. The East London Institute (1873) "sent" its
    graduates by training and releasing them to forty different outside missionary organizations
    at once, retaining no ongoing authority over any of them. The same table field, "sending
    body," records a college patronage clause in one row and an open placement service in the
    other. — *Table 5, "Sending body" column*: Emmanuel College Cambridge row versus East London
    Institute row.
