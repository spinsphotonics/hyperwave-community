# Dossier — Southern Baptist Theological Seminary (`southern-baptist-seminary`)

Note on sourcing: the Broadus *Memoir* (1893) is a compiled biography that both narrates events in Broadus's own voice and reprints, within quotation marks, verbatim extracts from Boyce's 1856 address and from the 1858 organizational discussions. Narrative statements of fact by Broadus (dates, numbers, names) are treated as PRIMARY-ADJACENT (a contemporary, participant-witness's own testimony — Broadus was one of the four professors elected in 1858) and cited PO where the field so allows; passages set off in quotation marks within the *Memoir* that reproduce Boyce's own words are cited as such. The *Three Changes* address and the *Abstract of Principles* are treated as fully PRIMARY.

## F — Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): The institution is named throughout the sources as "The Southern Baptist Theological Seminary" from its 1858 organization onward; no earlier or later name is recorded in the sources read. [sbts-q009, sbts-q022]
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): The organizing convention met at Greenville, South Carolina, beginning 1 May 1858, and its "discussions had been so free and full as to occupy five days," with the final vote on the organization believed unanimous; the *Memoir* elsewhere dates the Seminary's actual opening to the following autumn (1859). [sbts-q017]
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PS): James P. Boyce, John A. Broadus, Basil Manly Jr., and E. T. Winkler were the four men the 1858 convention "unanimously elected" as the founding professors; Winkler "promptly declined the election," so the Seminary opened with fewer than four. William Williams, present at the convention as the sole delegate from Georgia, was said afterward to have been in line for election but for Winkler's initial acceptance. [sbts-q018, sbts-q019]
- **F4 Place(s)** (PS): Greenville, South Carolina, was the founding location; during the organizing discussion Basil Manly Sr., presiding, cautioned Boyce against calling it "great" until its endowment was secured, indicating the Seminary was conceived from the outset with larger ambitions than its Greenville beginning. [sbts-q021] The seminary's later move to Louisville, Kentucky (1877) is outside this chapter's founding-era scope and not developed here beyond this note.
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): The *Abstract of Principles* (1858), the doctrinal statement every professor is required to sign, document_id `abstract-of-principles-1858`. Boyce's own 1856 address, *Three Changes in Theological Institutions*, proposed the Abstract as one of three needed reforms and is the argumentative charter behind the Seminary's design; document_id `three-changes-1856`. [sbts-q010, sbts-q004]
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): Boyce stated the object as producing "an abundance of able, sound, and faithful men to proclaim the gospel of Christ," and insisted that broadening access to theological training ("opened to all who would embrace them") was not in tension with "the education of the ministry a matter of the first importance." [sbts-q005, sbts-q001, sbts-q003] The *Memoir* describes the Seminary as organized "with the avowed view of giving theological instruction to young ministers in every grade of general education." [sbts-q022]
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PO): Boyce stated that existing theological seminaries had failed to draw students: "The theological seminary has not been a popular institution. But few have sought its advantages." [sbts-q006] His address was explicitly framed as proposing changes "which would enable them to fulfill more adequately... the hopes of their founders." [sbts-q004]
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): The *Abstract of Principles*, twenty articles covering Scripture, God, the Trinity, providence, election, the fall, the mediator, regeneration, repentance, faith, justification, sanctification, perseverance, the church, baptism, the Lord's Supper, the Lord's Day, liberty of conscience, the resurrection, and the judgment. Every professor "shall be a member of a regular Baptist church," and accepting a professorship is "engaging to teach in accordance with, and not contrary to" the Abstract, departure from which is "grounds for his resignation or removal by the Trustees." [sbts-q009, sbts-q010, sbts-q011] Boyce had proposed in 1856 that such a "declaration of doctrine" be required of professors specifically to guard against "erroneous and injurious instruction," per the *Memoir*'s summary of the address (this summary sentence is Broadus's paraphrase, not a direct Boyce quotation, and is not separately quote-recorded here as Boyce's own words for that reason).
- **F9 Antecedents the founders cite or imitate (earlier schools, documents, persons)** (PO): The Seminary's wholly elective course of study was "patterned after that which had for thirty years been in highly successful operation at the University of Virginia," where both Boyce and Broadus had studied or taught; the *Memoir* records this borrowing explicitly. No other founding-school model is named in the sources read. [sbts-q023]

## C — Curriculum
- **C1 Length of course** (PO): The regular course was designed to be completed "in one session" per school in the Seminary's original plan, though this was soon found impractical for Hebrew and Greek, which were "divided into a Junior and a Senior class"; a Full Graduate typically required "three or four years." A specially advanced post-graduate course was also proposed, extending "one or two years" beyond the ordinary course. [sbts-q007]
- **C2 Subjects, in the order taught** (PO): The Seminary comprised "eight distinct, and in a sense independent, departments of instruction, or schools": Biblical Introduction; Interpretation of the Old Testament; Interpretation of the New Testament; Systematic Theology; Polemic Theology and Apologetics; Homiletics; Church History; and Church Government and Pastoral Duties. [sbts-q023, sbts-q024]
- **C3 Structure (classes, years, terms)** (PO): Several of the eight schools were themselves split into an English-language general class and a more advanced class requiring the biblical languages, so that a student's course was "completely elective" rather than a fixed curriculum. [sbts-q023]
- **C4 Languages taught, and level required** (PO): Hebrew and Chaldee (with Hebrew Exegesis) in the Old Testament school, and New Testament Greek (with Greek Exegesis) in the New Testament school, each alongside a parallel English-language class requiring no biblical languages; Latin was used in the more advanced "Systematic Theology" track. Special departments (not required for the general degree) later added Arabic, Aramaic, Assyrian, Coptic, and Modern Greek. [sbts-q025, sbts-q026, sbts-q027]
- **C5 Set texts and authors** (PO): NOT FOUND for the founding period specifically (searched as above); the sources read do not name a set textbook list for 1858-1860.
- **C6 Preaching or practical instruction (form, frequency)** (PO): NOT FOUND for the founding period specifically (searched as above); Homiletics is named as one of the eight schools but no description of its exercises was located this session.
- **C7 Examinations and their form** (PO): Every school required "an intermediate and a final written examination, lasting nine or ten hours, with a brief oral examination in addition." A Full Graduate would have passed "more than twenty of these all-day written examinations," each scored out of 100, and "his paper must be worth at least seventy-five per cent on the whole in order to pass" (source's own scan reads "seventh-five"). [sbts-q030, sbts-q031]
- **C8 Degree, certificate, license, or ordination at completion** (PO): A separate diploma was given in each school to those who passed its examination; a student reaching only the English-language track in a given school received "a Certificate of Proficiency" rather than the full diploma. [sbts-q028, sbts-q029] J. A. Chambliss, of the first class, "remained two years, and was the Seminary's first Full Graduate." [sbts-q036]
- **C9 Weekly or daily timetable (table if given)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched as above); no weekly or daily class schedule for the founding sessions was located this session.

- **L6 Fees, board, and how students were supported** (PO): NOT FOUND for student fees specifically (searched as above). The institution itself was funded by a $70,000 subscription that Boyce personally raised as agent for the South Carolina Baptist Convention, "part... paid in cash, and the remainder... held in bonds bearing seven per cent interest."
- **L7 Number of students (with years)** (PS): First session (1859-60): 26 students, "a much larger number than had attended the first session of any other theological school in America," drawn from Virginia, North Carolina, South Carolina, Florida, Alabama, and Missouri. [sbts-q033] Second session: 36 students, "from twenty-six to thirty-six." [sbts-q037] By a Boyce letter of 1 December 1888 (Louisville era, well after founding but the only precise later figure located): "There are already one hundred and four students." [sbts-q038]

Taylor, is described as a man "whose rich promise was blighted by an early death," but no cause, date, or connection to Seminary conditions is given.

## T — Teachers
- **T1 Number and titles of teachers (with years)** (PS): Four professors elected in 1858 (Boyce, Broadus, Manly Jr., Winkler), with Winkler declining; the Seminary "had no president, but Professor Boyce was made Chairman of the Faculty" as originally organized (a title changed to "President" only in 1888, still with faculty government retained). [sbts-q018, sbts-q019, sbts-q032]
- **T2 Teachers' duties as set out in the charter** (PO): NOT FOUND as a distinct enumerated clause (searched as above); professors' primary obligation stated in the *Abstract of Principles* is doctrinal (see F8), not a description of duties.
- **T3 Forms of teacher–student contact (lecture, disputation, tutorial, table, correspondence, preaching class)** (PO): The written examination system (see C7) and the elective, small-class structure are the forms of instruction the sources describe; recitations mixed students of "very various qualifications" in the same class. No separate description of lecture format, tutorials, or a "table" tradition was located this session.
- **T4 Named examples of teacher–student interaction with source** (PS): Boyce is recorded corresponding personally with a former student turned missionary, Rev. W. S. Walker, at Shanghai, promising "We hope to send you other men from time to time." [sbts-q039]
- **T5 Teacher:student ratio if computable (show the two numbers and years)** (PS): Four professors (two accepting outright) to 26 students in session one (1859-60); computable as roughly 1:13 against the two who actually served that first year, or 1:6-7 against the eventual four. [sbts-q033, sbts-q018]

## M — Emphasis
- **M1 What the founders said mattered most (their words)** (PO): "We hold the education of the ministry a matter of the first importance to the churches of Christ." [sbts-q001] Boyce's stated aim was "an abundance of able, sound, and faithful men to proclaim the gospel of Christ." [sbts-q005]
- **M2 Distinctive stress (doctrine, piety, preaching, languages, evangelism, faith principle, self-support, other)** (PO): A combination of doctrinal fixity (the signed Abstract of Principles, F8) with unusually wide access (any grade of prior education, C2/A2) and a missionary outlook: the sources describe the elective plan as also meant to "help to train missionaries, such as may wish to translate the Scriptures into heathen languages." [sbts-q008]
- **M3 What the founders explicitly rejected or warned against** (PO): A member of the 1858 convention, Thomas Curtis, stated the founders' own priority as "bricks, books, brains," in that order, warning against spending "all their money for bricks" before providing books or securing capable teaching — advice the *Memoir* says the Seminary followed by declining to erect buildings before securing an endowment. [sbts-q020]
- **M4 Motto or watchword, with earliest source** (PS): NOT FOUND as a stated institutional motto (searched as above); the Commencement hymn "Soldiers of Christ, in truth arrayed" functioned as a recurring institutional watchword-in-song from the first Commencement (1860) onward, per the *Memoir*, though it is not called a "motto" in the source.
- **M5 Later leaders' restatements of the emphasis (with dates)** (PS): In a letter of 1 December 1888, Boyce (by then in Louisville) wrote of "so much missionary spirit in the Seminary, never so much in the churches at large," and pledged to "send you other men from time to time... in China, in Africa, in Mexico, in Brazil." [sbts-q040, sbts-q039]

## S — Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)** (PO): No separate licensing body or ordination council internal to the Seminary is described in the sources read. A "Seminary Missionary Society," run by students and funded by city Baptist churches, is recorded as active by 1888, running eight mission Sunday-schools with "about one thousand children" enrolled, and Boyce is recorded personally corresponding with and pledging to send more men to a former student serving as a missionary in Shanghai. [sbts-q041, sbts-q039]
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): China, Africa, Mexico, and Brazil are named as destinations to which Boyce pledged to send "other men from time to time" in his 1888 letter; individual missionaries named elsewhere in the sources (outside the Seminary's own alumni, as background) include missionaries to Italy and Japan, but these are general Southern Baptist mission-field references in the *Memoir*'s surrounding narrative, not specifically identified as Seminary graduates in the passages read. [sbts-q039]

## R — Fruit
- **R1-R4 Up to six named alumni or missionaries: name, dates, field, one-sentence outcome, source** (PS):
  1. J. A. Chambliss, of Alabama: entered with the first class (1859), "remained two years, and was the Seminary's first Full Graduate," afterward serving pastorates in Richmond and Charleston and, at the time the *Memoir* was written, East Orange, New Jersey. [sbts-q036]
  2. W. L. Ballard, of South Carolina: first name on the roll of the first entering class, "a plain country pastor, perhaps forty-five years old," whose one session of study was reported to have visibly improved his preaching in the churches that heard him afterward. [sbts-q035]
  3. Rev. W. S. Walker: a former student serving as a missionary at Shanghai, China, by 1888, the recipient of a personal letter from Boyce pledging further reinforcements from the Seminary. [sbts-q039]
  4. John W. Taylor, of Alabama: a first-generation student remembered in the *Memoir* as a man "of rare gifts and lovely character... whose rich promise was blighted by an early death"; no field of service or date of death is given in the passage read.
