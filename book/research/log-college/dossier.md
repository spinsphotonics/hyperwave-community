# Dossier — The Log College (`log-college`)

Note on sourcing (per the `04_task_tickets.md` extra-step for this slug): no charter,
statute, or other formal founding document for the Log College survives or was located
this session. Archibald Alexander's own 1845 book states this directly: "There seems to
be no written record of the existence of such an edifice... by any contemporary writer,
except in the Journal of Rev. George Whitefield" [alexander1845, quote_id
log-college-q010's surrounding sentence]. Per the ticket note, dossier field F5 is filled
with two descriptive sources in lieu of a charter: (1) George Whitefield's own 1739
journal entry describing his visit, fetched independently this session from Internet
Archive (document_id `whitefield-journal5-1739`); and (2) Archibald Alexander's
*Biographical Sketches of the Founder and Principal Alumni of the Log College* (1845),
which quotes the same Whitefield passage and adds further detail drawn from Synod and
Presbytery records, family papers, and family testimony (Elias Boudinot, Samuel Miller).
Both are treated as PRIMARY per the roster ("Key primary documents" cell for
`log-college` names both). This session's independent fetch of Whitefield's printed
journal (rather than relying on Alexander's quotation of it) cross-verifies Alexander's
transcription: allowing for the earlier printing's long-s typography and this OCR's
errors, the two texts agree almost word for word (compare quote_id log-college-q003 to
log-college-q010).

## F — Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): The building and school were called, "in contempt," simply "the College," or "The Log College" -- a name given by outsiders, not chosen by William Tennent himself. Whitefield's 1739 journal: "The Place wherein the young A'len fludy now is in Contempt call'd the College. It is a Log-Houfe..." [log-college-q003]. Alexander's 1845 book opens by naming the institution the same way: "That institution, we believe, was, what has been called the log college." [log-college-q007] Alexander adds, quoting Whitefield again, that the Journal "expressly says that it had obtained the name of 'THE COLLEGE'" and states that "the name College was given to the building out of contempt, by its enemies" [log-college-q010].
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): William Tennent Sr. received a call to the Presbyterian church at Neshaminy, Bucks County, Pennsylvania, in 1726, and remained there the rest of his life; Alexander states that "here, within a few steps of his own dwelling, he erected the building" that became the Log College [log-college-q020]. No single "opening day" or dedication date is recorded in either source read this session; the roster's "c.1726-27" [HIGH] reflects this. By the time of Whitefield's visit -- dated in this session's independently fetched primary journal (with an OCR misdate corrected by cross-reference, see `discrepancies.md` item 3) and confirmed by Alexander's own 1845 quotation as "Nov. 22. [1739.]" [log-college-q012; log-college-q001] -- the school had already sent out "Se''en or Eight" ministers and was training more [log-college-q004], so it had clearly been operating for some years by late 1739. Whitefield's visit closed on Friday, 23 November 1739, when he "Parted with dear Mr. Tennent" [log-college-q005].
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PS): William Tennent Sr. (c.1673-1746), an Irish-born, Episcopally-ordained-then-Presbyterian minister, was sole founder and, so far as either source establishes, sole regular instructor. Whitefield describes him after a visit to Philadelphia as "one Mr. Tennent, an old gray-headed disciple and soldier of Jesus Christ. He keeps an academy about twenty miles from Philadelphia, and has been blessed with four gracious sons, three of which have been, and still continue to be, eminently useful in the church of Christ" [log-college-q011]. Gilbert Tennent, the eldest son, assisted his father as an usher/tutor for about a year (1726-27) before his own ordination, but is described in Alexander's book as a pupil-turned-assistant rather than a co-founder or separate officer; Alexander records that "young men could be well prepared for the ministry at home, without going to distant colleges," because "Mr. William Tennent, the father, had been, as far as is known, the sole instructor of his son" [log-college-q016; log-college-q023].
- **F4 Place(s)** (PS): Neshaminy, Bucks County, Pennsylvania, "about twenty miles north of Philadelphia" [log-college-q008]; Whitefield's journal gives the same figure travelling from Trenton: "Set out for Nejhamini (twenty Miles diitant from Trent Town) where old A4r. Tennent lives, and keeps an Academy" [log-college-q001]. Alexander adds that the log building itself stood "within a few steps of" Tennent's own dwelling house [log-college-q020].
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): NOT FOUND -- no charter, statute, deed of foundation, or similar formal document was located or is known to survive (Alexander himself states no contemporary written record of the building exists apart from Whitefield's journal; see the sourcing note above). Per the project's ticket for this slug, F5 is instead answered by two descriptive documents: George Whitefield's 1739 journal entry (document_id `whitefield-journal5-1739`) and Archibald Alexander's 1845 *Biographical Sketches* (document_id `alexander1845`), chapter I especially. See `charter_abridged.md`, which assembles quoted descriptions from both in lieu of an abridged charter.
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): NOT FOUND as a sentence from William Tennent's own hand (he "never published anything," per Alexander's book, in the material read this session). What purpose is recorded comes from observers. Whitefield, on the spot in 1739, wrote that Tennent and his brethren, newly "appointed to be a Presbytery by the Synod," intended "breeding up gracious Youths, and fending them out from Time to Time into our Lord's Vineyard" [log-college-q002]. Alexander frames the whole book's occasion as answering "a similar curiosity, in regard to the first literary institution, above common schools, in the bounds of the Presbyterian church" [log-college-q007].
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PS): No source read this session records Tennent's own statement of the need he was answering. Alexander's narrative frames the wider need: before the Log College existed, "if a young man wished to become a minister in the Presbyterian Church, he must either repair to one of the New England colleges or go to Europe" -- and the first Presbyterian ministers in America had almost all been trained that way, in "the universities of Scotland," "Ireland," or "one of the New England colleges" [log-college-q009]. Alexander adds that Gilbert Tennent's successful 1726 licensure, "though he had received no diploma from any college," having instead "passed his trials before the mother Presbytery of Philadelphia, with great credit," showed for the first time in the colony that "young men could be well prepared for the ministry at home, without going to distant colleges" [log-college-q022; log-college-q016].
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session). Neither source gives a subscription formula, confession, or doctrinal test specifically tied to entry or instruction at the Log College.
- **F9 Antecedents the founders cite or imitate (earlier schools, documents, persons)** (PO/PS): NOT FOUND as a school or document William Tennent Sr. himself is recorded citing as a model; Alexander records only that Tennent was "brought up and received a liberal education" in Ireland, "probably... at Trinity College, Dublin," though this is Alexander's own inference ("it is probable"), not a stated antecedent, and is not separately quoted here as it falls outside this session's fetched excerpts. What the sources do establish clearly is the Log College's own place as an antecedent to later institutions: Alexander's Chapter VII states outright that "this humble institution was not only the germ of New Jersey College, but several other colleges... Jefferson, Hampden Sidney, and Washington College in Virginia; all which were founded and taught originally by students from Princeton" [log-college-q019], and names "the active friends and founders of Nassau Hall" as "the Tennents, Blairs, Finley, Smith, Rogers, Davies, and others who had received their education in the Log College, or in schools instituted by those who had been instructed there" [log-college-q018].

## A — Admission
- **A1 Minimum and maximum age** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session).
- **A2 Prior education required** (PO): No entrance examination or prior-education threshold is stated. What is established is a contrast with the wider Presbyterian practice of the time: before the Log College, prospective ministers needed a college or university education from Scotland, Ireland, or New England [log-college-q009], and Gilbert Tennent's 1726 licensure without any college diploma, on the strength of trials before the Presbytery alone, was itself the demonstration "that young men could be well prepared for the ministry at home, without going to distant colleges" [log-college-q016; log-college-q022].
- **A3 Testimony of conversion, character, or church membership required** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session).
- **A4 Entrance examination (subjects)** (PO): NOT FOUND for entry to the school itself. What is recorded is a Synod-imposed examination for licensure after leaving the school: in 1738 "the Synod adopted a rule, that no Presbytery should license any young man until he had passed an examination on his literary course before a committee of Synod," with separate northern and southern committees appointed to examine such candidates [log-college-q024]. This was an external check on the Log College's graduates, not an admission requirement of the College itself.
- **A5 Women: admitted? in what capacity?** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session). Every person named in connection with study, teaching, or licensure in the material read this session is male.
- **A6 Prior experience required (e.g., preaching, service)** (PO): NOT FOUND as a stated rule. Gilbert Tennent's case shows that a student could be licensed to preach directly out of the school, without prior separate ministerial experience, after passing the Presbytery's own trials [log-college-q016; log-college-q022].

## C — Curriculum
- **C1 Length of course** (PO): Not fixed or stated: Alexander writes plainly, "How many years they were occupied with these studies does not appear" [log-college-q021's surrounding sentence, same passage as log-college-q017; the "does not appear" clause itself is in that quote's immediate textual neighborhood -- see text/alexander1845.txt around log-college-q021].
- **C2 Subjects, in the order taught** (PO): "They were not only taught the classics, but studied divinity also; so that this institution was a theological seminary, as well as a college" [log-college-q017]. No order or sequence of subjects is given.
- **C3 Structure (classes, years, terms)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session). No division into classes, years, or terms is recorded; contemporary critics are quoted (via Alexander's narrative) as objecting that instruction came from "principally, by one teacher" in "this little paltry log cabin," unlike universities "furnished with so many professors."
- **C4 Languages taught, and level required** (PO): NOT FOUND as an explicit list; "the classics" (C2) implies Latin and Greek by the standard usage of the term in this period and context, but no source read this session states this directly for the Log College's own curriculum, so it is not asserted here beyond the word "classics" itself.
- **C5 Set texts and authors** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session).
- **C6 Preaching or practical instruction (form, frequency)** (PO): NOT FOUND as a description of in-school preaching exercises or homiletical training method. What is recorded is the outcome: a number of the school's students "were licensed by the Presbyteries, after undergoing such trials as were usually prescribed to candidates in Scotland and Ireland" [same passage as log-college-q017/q021], and some "became eminent in the church," "distinguished as powerful and evangelical preachers."
- **C7 Examinations and their form** (PO): No examination internal to the Log College is described. The external examination imposed on its graduates by the wider Synod from 1738 onward is described at log-college-q024 (see A4 above): a literary-course examination before one of two Synod committees (northern and southern), required before any Presbytery could license a candidate.
- **C8 Degree, certificate, license, or ordination at completion** (PO): No diploma or degree was conferred by the Log College itself. Gilbert Tennent, its first pupil-turned-tutor, "had received no diploma from any college, yet he passed his trials before the mother Presbytery of Philadelphia, with great credit to himself" [log-college-q022] -- students proceeded directly to Presbytery licensure trials rather than to a school-conferred credential.
- **C9 Weekly or daily timetable (table if given)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session). No table or schedule survives in either source.

- **L2 Residence: where students lived; with whom** (PO): The school building stood "within a few steps of" William Tennent's own dwelling house at Neshaminy [log-college-q020], and Whitefield, visiting, was received "to old Mr. Tennent's, who entertain'd us like one of the ancient Patriarchs" -- describing Tennent's household, not a separate dormitory [log-college-q006]. - **L8 Health, deaths, or hardship recorded among students** (PS): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session) among students specifically. The founder's own death is recorded: William Tennent Sr. died at his own house in Neshaminy; Alexander gives "the exact date of his death... May 6, 1746, aged 73," communicated to him by the Rev. Dr. Samuel Miller, who had it "transcribed... from his tombstone" -- though an earlier printed source Alexander cites and rejects had given 1743 (see `discrepancies.md` item 2) [log-college-q015].

## T — Teachers
- **T1 Number and titles of teachers (with years)** (PS): One regular teacher: William Tennent Sr. himself. Alexander states that "Mr. William Tennent, the father, had been, as far as is known, the sole instructor of his son" (Gilbert) [log-college-q023], and Whitefield independently corroborates that Tennent alone "keeps an academy" [log-college-q011]. Gilbert Tennent assisted informally for about a year (1726-27) before his own licensure and ordination, but no source read this session gives him a separate title as a teacher of the school (see F3).
- **T2 Teachers' duties as set out in the charter** (PO): NOT FOUND -- no charter exists (see F5); no other document read this session sets out teaching duties in formal terms.
- **T3 Forms of teacher-student contact (lecture, disputation, tutorial, table, correspondence, preaching class)** (PO): The clearest description of contact is not academic instruction but hospitality: Whitefield's party, after the public preaching, "went to old Mr. Tennent, who en-tertain'd us like one of the ancient Patriarchs," and "spent the Evening in concerting what Meafures had befi be taken for promoting our dear Lord's Kingdom" [log-college-q006] -- a household setting for both fellowship and practical planning, consistent with a single teacher instructing pupils under his own roof, though no source read this session describes a lecture, disputation, or tutorial format specifically.
- **T4 Named examples of teacher-student interaction with source** (PS): NOT FOUND as a specific classroom anecdote in the material read this session, beyond the household hospitality described at T3.
- **T5 Teacher:student ratio if computable (show the two numbers and years)** (PS): NOT FOUND -- no enrollment figure is given for any year (see L7), so no ratio can be computed; contemporary critics quoted in Alexander's narrative note only, without a number, that instruction came "principally, by one teacher" (see C3).

## M — Emphasis
- **M1 What the founders said mattered most (their words)** (PO): NOT FOUND as William Tennent's own words (he left no publications, per Alexander). What survives is the outside assessment quoted throughout this dossier: Whitefield's description of the log building as resembling "the Schools of the old Prophets," whose "Habitations were mean" and who "fought not great Things for themfelves" -- set against "moft of our publick Universities," which "are all glorious without" [log-college-q003]; Alexander reproduces the same passage in his own book, adding the school "was, in a noble sense, a college; a fountain, from which... proceeded streams of blessings to the church" in his own narrative voice rather than in Tennent's words [log-college-q010].
- **M2 Distinctive stress (doctrine, piety, preaching, languages, evangelism, faith principle, self-support, other)** (PO): The material read this session stresses effectual preaching and evangelistic sending over institutional apparatus: Whitefield notes the school's aim was "breeding up gracious Youths, and fending them out from Time to Time into our Lord's Vineyard" [log-college-q002], and Alexander frames its significance entirely by its pupils' later usefulness as revival preachers rather than by any scholarly apparatus the school itself possessed (see F9, R below).
- **M3 What the founders explicitly rejected or warned against** (PO): NOT FOUND as a statement from Tennent himself. Alexander's narrative records what the school's *critics* within the Synod rejected -- instruction by "principally, by one teacher" in "this little paltry log cabin" as inadequate compared to university training (see C3) -- a criticism directed at the school, not a position the founder is recorded taking.
- **M4 Motto or watchword, with earliest source** (PS): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session). No motto is recorded; the nearest thing is the outsiders' derisive nickname itself, "the College" (see F1).
- **M5 Later leaders' restatements of the emphasis (with dates)** (PS): NOT FOUND as a restatement by a named later leader of the Log College's own emphasis, distinct from Alexander's 1845 narrative voice itself, in the material read this session.

## S — Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)** (PO): Licensure and ordination ran through the regular Presbyterian courts, not a mechanism internal to the Log College. Whitefield records in 1739 that "Mr. Te7i~nent and his Brethren are appointed to be a Presbytery by the Synod, fo that they intend breeding up gracious Youths, and fending them out from Time to Time into our Lord's Vineyard" [log-college-q002] -- i.e., the newly erected (New Brunswick) Presbytery, composed of Tennent and like-minded ministers, held the licensing power directly, rather than deferring to the wider Synod. From 1738 the wider Synod required its own literary examination of Log College graduates before any Presbytery could license them (see A4/C7) [log-college-q024], a restriction the school's friends "were exceedingly averse to," per Alexander's narrative, precisely because it targeted the Log College's own pupils.
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): NOT FOUND as a specific list of places graduates were sent, in the material read this session; the College of New Jersey material (F9) shows former Log College men becoming founders and faculty of that institution and, through its own graduates, of Jefferson, Hampden-Sidney, and Washington College in Virginia [log-college-q018; log-college-q019] -- lineage rather than a first-generation sending destination.
- **S3 Numbers sent, by period, with source** (PS): By 22 November 1739, per Whitefield's own eyewitness count: "From this defpifcd Place Se''en or Eight worthy Minifters of Jesus have lately been fent forth; more are almofl ready to be fcnt" [log-college-q004].
- **S4 Support on the field (salary, faith principle, self-support)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session).
- **S5 Deaths, martyrdoms, or casualties recorded, with source** (PS): NOT FOUND among those sent out from the Log College, in the material read this session (see L8 for the founder's own death, which is not a casualty of sending).
- **S6 Instructions given to those sent (title, date, document_id)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: whitefield-journal5-1739; alexander1845 excerpts fetched this session).

## R — Fruit
- **R1-R4** (PS): (1) William Tennent Sr. (c.1673-1746), founder and sole regular teacher; Whitefield's 1739 assessment: "an old gray-headed disciple and soldier of Jesus Christ," blessed "with four gracious sons, three of which have been, and still continue to be, eminently useful in the church of Christ" [log-college-q011]. (2) Gilbert Tennent, eldest son, the school's first pupil (and briefly its tutor), licensed to preach in 1726 "though he had received no diploma from any college," on the strength of his trials before the Presbytery of Philadelphia [log-college-q022]. (3) Unnamed "Se'en or Eight worthy Ministers of Jesus" sent out from the school by November 1739, per Whitefield's eyewitness count, with "more... almost ready to be sent" [log-college-q004]. (4) Jonathan Dickinson and Aaron Burr -- named by Alexander among "the active friends and founders of Nassau Hall" (the College of New Jersey), alongside "the Tennents, Blairs, Finley, Smith, Rogers, Davies, and others who had received their education in the Log College, or in schools instituted by those who had been instructed there" [log-college-q018]; Dickinson served as the College of New Jersey's first president and Burr as its second, per Alexander's narrative.
