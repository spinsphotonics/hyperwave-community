# Dossier — Student Volunteer Movement for Foreign Missions (`student-volunteer-movement`)

Note on sourcing: this institution is a student sending movement, not a residential school.
Per the roster row, "the declaration card... and the watchword are a one-line charter." No
formal SVM constitution text was located as a digitized full document this session (the
Princeton Foreign Missionary Society's constitution is quoted only in part, at second hand,
inside Wilder's 1891 retrospective address). Consequently many of the template's Curriculum
(C), Common Life (L), and some Admission (A) fields, written for a residential school, are
NOT FOUND or not applicable to a movement whose "students" are its members across hundreds
of institutions, not residents of one campus. This mirrors the honest gap already logged in
the `abcfm` chapter for the same reason. Four documents were fetched and read in full this
session: the 1891 Cleveland convention report (the fullest primary account of the Movement's
origin, organization, and first achievements), Mott's short 1892 History (which fixes the
exact 1892 wording change of the declaration), Mott's 1900 book on the Watchword, and the
1906 Executive Committee report to the Fifth Convention (updated statistics). A fifth
document (Wilder's 1924 pamphlet) was fetched but its OCR was too corrupted to yield usable
quotations on a targeted search; it is logged in sources.csv but not cited below.

## F — Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): The movement was named the "Student Volunteer Movement for Foreign Missions" when its permanent Executive Committee was organized in 1888-89, restricting membership to students. [svm-q010, svm-q002]
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): The originating event was the Mount Hermon student conference, July 1886 (Moody's summer Bible school); a hundred men there signed a missionary declaration by the close of the conference; the Movement was "formally organized" as a permanent body with an Executive Committee in 1888, beginning its regular work in January 1889; the Watchword was adopted as one of the Executive Committee's first acts in 1888; the wording of the declaration card itself was formally changed on 14 July 1892. [svm-q002, svm-q004, svm-q010, svm-q013, svm-q001]
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PO): Robert P. Wilder of Princeton College was the student who first called together those "thinking seriously" of foreign service at Mount Hermon in 1886, and he and John N. Forman (also of Princeton) were sent as the traveling deputation to the colleges in 1886-87; John R. Mott was appointed the YMCA's representative on the Executive Committee formed in 1888-89 and "has held this position ever since" (as of the 1892 History); Wilder represented the Missionary Alliances on the same committee until 1891, succeeded by Robert E. Speer, then in 1892 by D. Willard Lyon. [svm-q002, svm-q019, svm-q017, svm-q018]
- **F4 Place(s)** (PO): Mount Hermon, Massachusetts (originating conference, July 1886); the Movement's later international conventions were held at Cleveland, Ohio (1891, this chapter's principal source) and elsewhere; its office in the 1889-92 period is not given a city in the material read this session. [svm-q002]
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): No single constitutive charter document was located and fetched this session. The two texts that together function as the Movement's founding record are (a) the volunteer declaration card, in its original 1886 wording and its revised 1892 wording, and (b) the Executive Committee's own "Report of the Executive Committee," presented to the First International Convention, Cleveland, 1891 (document_id cleveland1891), which is the fullest primary narrative of origin, extension, organization, and aims. This chapter's abridged charter is built from (b), with the declaration wordings from document_id motthistory1892 (1892) reproduced within it. [svm-q001, svm-q002]
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): At the originating 1886 conference, Arthur T. Pierson's address argued "that \"all should go, and go to all\"" -- the premise the Executive Committee's own history credits as the "key-note which set many men to thinking and praying." [svm-q003]
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PO): The same address is the only explicit statement of underlying rationale located this session; the Executive Committee's history frames the whole 1886 conference as answering a felt want for many more foreign missionary "workers," reported at the "meeting of the ten nations" a week later, though that meeting itself was not separately quoted for a crisis-statement this session. [svm-q003]
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): The Movement had no confession of faith as such; its sole membership act was subscribing the volunteer declaration, in its old form ("I am willing and desirous, God permitting, to become a foreign missionary") or, from 14 July 1892, its new form ("It is my purpose, if God permit, to become a foreign missionary"). [svm-q001]
- **F9 Antecedents the founders cite or imitate (earlier schools, documents, persons)** (PO): The Movement's own 1891 report explicitly compares its 1886-87 deputation plan to the British "Cambridge Band," whose tour of the British universities before its members went to China it names as the model; separately, Robert P. Wilder's own retrospective address traces his personal formula back further, to the Princeton Foreign Missionary Society he helped found around 1883-84, whose constitution bound members by an earlier-worded covenant: "We, the undersigned, declare ourselves willing and desirous, God permitting, to go to the unevangelized portions of the world." [svm-q005, svm-q006]

- **A4 Entrance examination (subjects)** (PO): None; membership was by signing the declaration, not examination. The rigorous examination described in the sources belongs to the denominational mission boards, at the point of actually being sent, not to entry into the Movement. [svm-q011]

## L — Common life
- **L1-L5, L8** (PO): Not applicable; the Movement had no residential common life. NOT FOUND.
- **L6 Fees, board, and how students were supported** (PO): The Movement itself charged no fees; its own report records that it promoted a plan under which colleges and theological seminaries undertook to support their own missionaries under their respective denominational Boards, with at least 40 colleges and 32 seminaries adopting the plan in whole or part and at least $30,000 raised above previous giving in two years. [svm-q009]
- **L7 Number of students (with years)** (PS): 6,200 volunteers on the rolls at the time of the First International Convention (1891), scattered through 350 institutions; the Movement's own achievements list records "6,000 young men and women" having signed the declaration by early 1891. [svm-q007; PS: cleveland1891, statistics summary at the convention's Prefatory Note, giving 6,000 volunteers and 5 years since Mt. Hermon]

## T — Teachers
- **T1 Number and titles of teachers (with years)** (PS): The Movement's permanent Executive Committee (from January 1889) had three members, one appointed by each of the College YMCA, the College YWCA, and the Inter-Seminary Missionary Alliance (later joined by the Canadian Intercollegiate Missionary Alliance): John R. Mott represented the YMCA continuously from 1888; Robert P. Wilder represented the Missionary Alliances until 1891, then Robert E. Speer, then (1892) D. Willard Lyon. Three paid secretaries served under the Committee -- Traveling, Corresponding, and Editorial -- the Corresponding Secretary post held by William H. Hannum until he sailed for India in 1890, then by Walter J. Clark. [svm-q017, svm-q018, svm-q016]
- **T2-T5** (PO/PS): No formal "duties" charter clause was located for these secretaries beyond the functional description already quoted in T1 (organizing, corresponding, editing); no teacher-student ratio applies to a movement of this kind. NOT FOUND / not applicable.

## M — Emphasis
- **M1 What the founders said mattered most (their words)** (PO): Arthur T. Pierson's founding address argued "all should go, and go to all"; the Movement's own later self-description states plainly that, because it is "a movement for foreign missions, the principal proof of its efficiency is to be found in the going forth of its members to the foreign mission field" -- not any other measure of activity. [svm-q003, svm-q015]
- **M2 Distinctive stress (doctrine, piety, preaching, languages, evangelism, faith principle, self-support, other)** (PO): Evangelism defined narrowly and deliberately: Robert E. Speer, addressing the 1891 Convention on the Watchword, insisted the Movement's aim was neither "the conversion of the world" nor "the Christianization... [nor] the civilization of the world," but the presenting of the Gospel to every soul so that the responsibility for its reception "shall...rest on each man's head for himself." [svm-q012]
- **M3 What the founders explicitly rejected or warned against** (PO): Speer's address explicitly rejects two common misreadings of the Watchword -- that it means the world's conversion, or its Christianization/civilization -- as not "the ambitions of the Student Volunteer Movement." [svm-q012]
- **M4 Motto or watchword, with earliest source** (PS): The Watchword, "The Evangelization of the World in this Generation," was adopted by the Executive Committee as one of its first acts on the Movement's formal organization in 1888, per Mott's own 1900 account; the volunteer declaration itself functioned as a second, personal watchword-like formula, changed from "I am willing and desirous, God permitting, to become a foreign missionary" to "It is my purpose, if God permit, to become a foreign missionary" on 14 July 1892. [svm-q013, svm-q001]
- **M5 Later leaders' restatements of the emphasis (with dates)** (PS): Mott's 1900 book restates and defends the Watchword at length as "at once concise, comprehensive, definite" -- the fullest restatement located this session, dated to the book's 1900/1901 printing. [SECONDARY-adjacent primary restatement: mott1900, not separately extracted as a further quote beyond svm-q013's surrounding context this session]

## S — Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)** (PO): The Movement itself did not send or ordain; a volunteer wishing to go abroad was directed to "apply to the Board of Foreign Missions of some evangelical denomination," whose examination of candidates the 1891 report calls "most thorough." The Movement's proper measure of success, per its own later report, was simply "the going forth of its members to the foreign mission field" under those boards. [svm-q011, svm-q015]
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): UNVERIFIED for a clean per-country breakdown this session. The 1891 report does tabulate "Distribution of volunteers who have sailed according to fields" by continent and region (Africa subdivided East/North/South/West, etc.), but the table's OCR is broken across many short lines and individual cell numbers were not reliably recoverable; not quoted. See discrepancies.md.
- **S3 Numbers sent, by period, with source** (PS): By the First International Convention (1891): "at least 320" volunteers had already sailed, against "6,000" who had signed the declaration since 1886. By January 1, 1906 (Fifth Convention report): "2,953 volunteers" had sailed to the mission field, with roughly one-third being women and not less than fifty denominations represented. [svm-q007, svm-q008, svm-q014]
- **S4 Support on the field (salary, faith principle, self-support)** (PO): Volunteers sailed "under the various missionary agencies" (i.e., their denominational boards), not under any faith-mission or self-support principle of the SVM's own; separately, the Movement promoted colleges and seminaries directly supporting their own missionaries under those same boards. [svm-q008, svm-q009]
- **S5 Deaths, martyrdoms, or casualties recorded, with source** (PS): UNVERIFIED this session. The 1891 report's statistical table appears to include a "Deceased" row among its twelve categories of volunteer status, but (as with S2) the OCR breaks the table into isolated digits on separate lines and no reliable, quotable figure was recovered; do not state a casualty number in the chapter. See discrepancies.md.
- **S6 Instructions given to those sent (title, date, document_id)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: cleveland1891, mott1900, motthistory1892, firsttwodecades1906). The Movement issued no instructions of its own to departing volunteers comparable to the ABCFM's 1812 Instructions; the boards under which volunteers actually sailed would have issued any such instructions, outside the material read this session.
