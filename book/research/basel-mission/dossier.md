# Dossier — Basel Mission and its seminary (`basel-mission`)

Note on sourcing (read this before the fields below): the Basel Mission's own primary German
documents — the *Hausordnung* (house rules) of 1816 and the *Evangelisches Missions-Magazin* —
were located on Internet Archive and bmarchives.org but were **not fetched or translated this
session**, per the task's explicit instruction to search first for an English-language scholarly
account. That search succeeded: Linda Maria Ratschiller Nasim's 2023 Open Access book chapter is
a modern, rigorously footnoted English-language history of the Basel Mission seminary that
quotes short phrases directly from the archival Hausordnung documents (cited there by Basel
Mission Archive shelfmark, e.g. "BMA, Q-9.31") and quotes at length from a missionary's own
manuscript autobiography. Passages given in quotation marks in Ratschiller's text, and
attributed by her footnotes to a named primary source, are treated as PRIMARY at second hand and
cited PO. Ratschiller's own analytical/narrative sentences are cited [SECONDARY: document_id,
location]. No claim below states more than these two English secondary sources actually say.
This dossier is markedly thinner than `geneva-academy` or `serampore-college` on the
day-to-day administrative and curricular detail a full Hausordnung translation would supply
(exact daily timetable, exact admission age, named house officers) — those gaps are marked NOT
FOUND rather than guessed at, and are flagged in discrepancies.md as the natural next step for a
reader of German.

## F — Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): The mission society is called the Basel Mission (Basel Evangelical Missionary Society); its training house is called the Basel Mission seminary throughout the sources read. [ratschiller-chapter2023, para. under 2.1.2]
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): "Created in 1815, during an evangelical revival on the European continent, the Basel Mission resulted from the synergy of the city's bourgeoisie and devout Pietists." The mission "initiated a seminary for the education of overseas evangelists in Basel in 1815" [Ratschiller's 2023 chapter states 1815 for the seminary itself; the roster's known-facts sheet instead distinguishes mission (1815) from seminary opening (1816) — see discrepancies.md item 1]. [basel-mission-q022, basel-mission-q001]
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PS): The mission arose from the Pietist *Deutsche Christentumsgesellschaft* (German Society for Christianity), founded in Basel in 1780; its leadership from 1815 was "a self-perpetuating circle of men, called the Committee, that usually had about twelve members, mostly academics, politicians, jurists, merchants, bankers and manufacturers originating from patrician families." Christian Gottlieb Blumhardt "was one of the founding members of the Basel Mission and the first Inspector from 1815 until his death in 1838." [basel-mission-q002, basel-mission-q017]
- **F4 Place(s)** (PS): Basel, Switzerland. [general background throughout both sources; not separately quoted]
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): The seminary's *Hausordnung* (house rules) is repeatedly cited by Ratschiller from Basel Mission Archive shelfmarks (e.g. "Plan der neuen Gestaltung der Hausordnung des Missionshauses in Basel, Mai 1860, BMA, Q-9.31"; "Haus-Ordnung der evangelischen Missions-Anstalt zu Basel, Basel 1888, BMA, X.III.12"), but no version of this German document was itself located and fetched this session. The dossier's F5 document is therefore the secondary chapter that quotes it: document_id `ratschiller-chapter2023`. See discrepancies.md item 2.
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): NOT FOUND as a direct quotation from the founders themselves this session. Ratschiller states, in her own words, that "the founders of the mission seminary in Basel invoked the scriptural injunction to go into the world and share the gospel of Jesus Christ" — this is her paraphrase, not a quoted founding document, and is cited [SECONDARY] accordingly. [basel-mission-q021]
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PO): NOT FOUND as the founders' own words. Ratschiller's webinar text states, in her own analysis: "The founding members of the mission seminary understood their evangelising aspirations as a form of atonement for the transatlantic slave trade initiated and controlled by Europeans." This is a historian's interpretive claim, not a quotation from a founding document, and is cited [SECONDARY]. [basel-mission-q023]
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): NOT FOUND as a specific confessional text or subscription clause. Ratschiller states the Basel Mission "was never an organisation of merely one denomination or church," drawing on both the Reformed tradition of Swiss Protestant churches and the Lutheran tradition of Wurttemberg — a description of doctrinal breadth, not a subscription requirement. [SECONDARY narrative, ratschiller-chapter2023, para. under "Worldwide Webs"; not separately quoted this session]

## A — Admission
- **A1 Minimum and maximum age** (PO): No fixed minimum/maximum age rule was located, but one named case is documented: Rudolf Fisch, the Basel Mission's first doctor, "applied to join the Basel Mission seminary as soon as he turned eighteen and started his missionary training in August of 1875." This is a single example, not a stated rule. [basel-mission-q019]
- **A2 Prior education required** (PO): No formal prior-education requirement is stated; the Committee "favoured a homogenous group of pupils, believing that uniformity in beliefs, education and experience contributed to the predictability and efficiency of training," and most pupils were craftsmen, peasants and tradesmen without university training. [basel-mission-q006; SECONDARY narrative on social composition, ratschiller-chapter2023, para. under "Worldwide Webs," not separately quoted]
- **A3 Testimony of conversion, character, or church membership required** (PO): "The decisive criterion for acceptance to the Basel Mission seminary was a candidate's convincing account of spiritual rebirth that signalled complete emotional surrender to the larger Pietist cause." "Applicants had to describe the exact moment of their own conversion and the authenticity of the account had to be confirmed by a witness, usually the candidate's own local minister, who was known to the Basel Mission." The Committee cited a lack of "moral integrity" and "divine vocation" as the most common reasons for rejecting applicants. [basel-mission-q007, basel-mission-q008, basel-mission-q010]
- **A4 Entrance examination (subjects)** (PO): No academic entrance examination is described; admission turned on the conversion narrative above. Numerically, "between 1880 and 1914, between 30 and 80 men applied for a place at the Basel Mission seminary each year, of which between 14 and 25 were admitted as pupils." [basel-mission-q009]
- **A5 Women: admitted? in what capacity?** (PO): "Women were not included in seminary training and ordination"; women involved in Basel Mission work in West Africa (single women and missionary wives) came from outside the seminary's own training pipeline. [basel-mission-q015]
- **A6 Prior experience required (e.g., preaching, service)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: ratschiller-chapter2023, ratschiller-webinar2025).

## C — Curriculum
- **C1 Length of course** (PO): "Their education took a minimum of five years to complete." Only about a third of admitted pupils completed the training. [basel-mission-q005, basel-mission-q014]
- **C2 Subjects, in the order taught** (PO): The five-year course "included Latin, Greek, Hebrew, English, German, medicine, arithmetic, geometry, physics, geography, history, pedagogics, anthropology, Bible classes and catechism." From 1845, "all mission contenders were given medical lectures that followed Pietist tenets of healing as part of their five-year course." No teaching sequence/order is stated. [basel-mission-q005, basel-mission-q016]
- **C3 Structure (classes, years, terms)** (PO): NOT FOUND beyond the five-year overall length (C1); no year-by-year breakdown located.
- **C4 Languages taught, and level required** (PO): Latin, Greek, Hebrew, English and German are named as taught subjects; Johann Blumhardt (nephew of the first Inspector) "worked at the mission seminary from 1830 to 1837, teaching Hebrew, mathematics, physics and chemistry." No required proficiency level is stated. [basel-mission-q005, basel-mission-q018]

## T — Teachers

## S — Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)** (PO): NOT FOUND as a formal sending procedure this session; what is documented is that graduates of the Basel seminary went on to serve other societies: "More than one hundred missionaries from the Anglican Church Missionary Society received their training in the seminary in Basel between 1819 and 1858," and "the first young men to complete their training in Basel worked for British and Dutch mission societies, mainly the Church Missionary Society." [basel-mission-q004, basel-mission-q024]
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): By 1821, the Committee "had decided to establish their own mission stations abroad," reaching Russia, China, India and Africa, "most notably on the Gold Coast in 1828 and in Cameroon in 1885." One quoted destination for CMS-trained Basel graduates is implicit in "a future consecrated Bishop of Jerusalem" (unnamed in the source). [basel-mission-q004; SECONDARY narrative on mission fields, ratschiller-chapter2023, para. under 2.1.2, not separately quoted this session]
- **S3 Numbers sent, by period, with source** (PS): The CMS-training figure above ("more than one hundred...between 1819 and 1858") is the only cumulative sending number located and quoted this session. [basel-mission-q004]
