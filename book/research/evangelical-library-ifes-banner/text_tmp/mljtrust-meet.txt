#

David Martyn Lloyd-Jones (20 December 1899-1 March 1981) stands as one of the
most influential figures in 20th-century Reformed evangelicalism. A Welsh
Protestant minister, preacher, and medical doctor, he served as minister of
Westminster Chapel in London for nearly three decades.

### Early Life and Ministry

Born in Cardiff and raised in Llangeitho, Ceredigion, Lloyd-Jones grew up in a
region steeped in Welsh Methodist revival history, notably as the site of
Daniel Rowland's ministry. His father worked as a grocer, and of his two
brothers, Harold tragically died in the 1918 flu pandemic, while Vincent later
became a High Court judge.

His early career path led him through London grammar school (1914-1917) to St
Bartholomew's Hospital, where he trained in medicine. By 1921, he had secured
a prestigious position as assistant to the Royal Physician, Sir Thomas Horder.
After obtaining his MD from London University and membership in the Royal
College of Physicians, Lloyd-Jones experienced a profound calling to preach.
This spiritual conviction led him, in 1927, to return to Wales with his new
wife, Bethan Phillips. Together with Bethan, who would later bear their two
daughters Elizabeth and Ann, he accepted a ministerial position at a church in
Aberavon (Port Talbot).

### Westminster Chapel

In 1939, Lloyd-Jones returned to London as associate pastor of Westminster
Chapel, serving alongside G. Campbell Morgan. His appointment coincided with
the outbreak of World War II, during which he relocated his family to
Haslemere, Surrey. That same year, he assumed the presidency of the Inter-
Varsity Fellowship of Students (now the Universities and Colleges Christian
Fellowship UK). Following Morgan's retirement in 1943, Lloyd-Jones became the
sole Pastor of Westminster Chapel.

His ministry was distinguished by masterful expository preaching that drew
thousands to Sunday services and Friday evening Bible studies. His methodical
approach to Scripture saw him devoting months, sometimes years, to verse-by-
verse exposition of biblical chapters. These hour-long sermons particularly
attracted university students from across London. The Westminster Record
preserved these messages in print, providing wider access to his teaching.

### Later Life and Legacy

Following a major operation, Lloyd-Jones retired from Westminster Chapel in
1968. He dedicated his remaining years to publishing his sermons, counseling
ministers, correspondence, and conference speaking. His magnum opus emerged as
a 14-volume commentary on Romans, with the first volume appearing in 1970.

Though long resident in England, Lloyd-Jones maintained strong ties to his
Welsh heritage, regularly addressing the Evangelical Movement of Wales in both
English and Welsh. His final sermon was delivered at Barcombe Baptist Chapel
on 7 June 1980. On St David's Day, 1 March 1981, he passed away peacefully at
his Ealing home. His burial took place in Newcastle Emlyn, near Cardigan,
followed by a memorial service at Westminster Chapel on 6 April.

Lloyd-Jones's influence continues through numerous publications about his life
and work, most notably Iain Murray's comprehensive two-volume biography.

### A Note on His Name

Dr. Lloyd-Jones's given name, Martyn, reflects its Welsh origins — a form of
the Latin Martinus that has been used in Wales for centuries and remains
distinct from the more common English rendering, Martin. The two are
frequently confused, and searches for "Martin Lloyd-Jones" are among the most
common ways new listeners first encounter his ministry.

His surname is correctly written as a hyphenated compound: Lloyd-Jones. The
unhyphenated form — Lloyd Jones — appears widely in informal and digital
contexts and refers to the same person.

His full legal name was David Martyn Lloyd-Jones, though he was known
throughout his ministry simply as Dr. Lloyd-Jones, or by those closest to him
as The Doctor — an affectionate reference to his earlier vocation as a
physician under the Royal Physician Sir Thomas Horder.

The initialism MLJ is used broadly in Reformed evangelical circles to refer to
him and to the Trust that bears his name.
