# Dossier — Bristol Baptist Academy (`bristol-baptist-academy`)

Note on sourcing: no single self-contained "charter" document (in the sense of a founding
statute with numbered articles) has been located digitized for this institution. The closest
primary analogue is *An Account of the Bristol Education Society, Begun Anno 1770* (Bristol,
1776), whose "outlines of this most excellent institution" section states the Society's design
and rules; this is the document used in `charter_abridged.md`. Edward Terrill's own 1679 deed of
gift — the true founding instrument of the studentship, and the document the roster names first
— has not been located as an independently digitized primary text; it is known to this dossier
only through quotation, by two different secondary/documentary sources (an 1847 editorial notice
and a 1971 scholarly article), of what both present as the deed's own wording. Both quotations
agree closely but not word-for-word (see discrepancies.md). Per the geneva-academy precedent
(Borgeaud's quotations of the Genevan Registres), these quoted-in-quotation-marks fragments are
treated here as primary-adjacent and cited for PRIMARY-ONLY fields, while the surrounding
editorial/scholarly narrative is treated as secondary. Fields marked NOT FOUND below reflect a
genuine absence in the five documents this session fetched and read in full (or in the case of
the two Baptist Quarterly articles, in full as PDF text), not a failure to search.

## F — Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): The studentship established under Edward Terrill's 1679 bequest was administered from 1720 by Bernard Foskett as an "academy" attached to Broadmead Baptist Church, Bristol; the roster and secondary sources call it interchangeably the Bristol Academy / Baptist Academy at Bristol. In 1770 the Bristol Education Society was founded "in aid of" that academy, and Rippon (1795) records that Caleb Evans, as the Society's founder, "gave the name of The Bristol Education Society" to the enlarged institution. [bba-q003, bba-q010] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847; rippon-1795-essay]
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): Edward Terrill executed his deed of gift in 1679 (dated, per Moon 1971 quoting the deed's own dateline, "3rd June, 1679"); the bequest did not come into effective use until Bernard Foskett's appointment in 1720, and the first student under Foskett, Thomas Rogers, was admitted "5th of November, 1720" per the trustees' own minute. The Bristol Education Society was "formed in 1770." [bba-q003, bba-q004, bba-q007, bba-q022] [SECONDARY: moon-1971-caleb-evans] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847; rippon-1795-essay]
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PS): Edward Terrill, a ruling elder of Broadmead church, provided the founding bequest. Bernard Foskett, appointed 1720, was the first tutor under whom the bequest operated effectively; Hugh Evans succeeded him in 1758, and Hugh's son Caleb Evans, described by Rippon as the Society's "father," founded the Bristol Education Society in 1770. James Newton was invited in as classical tutor once the Society enlarged the work. [bba-q003, bba-q010, bba-q014] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: rippon-1795-essay]
- **F4 Place(s)** (PS): Bristol, England; the academy and Society were based at Broadmead Baptist Church (the roster and secondary sources also note a later move to North Street and then Stokes Croft, both outside this dossier's fetched material — NOT FOUND with a primary date this session).
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): *An Account of the Bristol Education Society, Begun Anno 1770* (Bristol, 1776), English, document_id `bes-account-1776`, is the document used for `charter_abridged.md`. Edward Terrill's own 1679 deed of gift is the earlier and more properly "founding" instrument but has not been located as an independently digitized primary text this session; its wording is known only through quotation in `terrill-records-1847` (1847 editorial notice) and `moon-1971-caleb-evans` (1971 article, itself quoting an unspecified prior source, likely Dakin 1929 per its footnote 21). [bba-q001, bba-q005, bba-q021]
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): Terrill's deed, as quoted, gives the purpose "for the glory of God, and the propagation of the gospel of our Lord Jesus Christ, and for the true love and affection he hath and boareth unto the congregation of which he is a member." The 1770 Society's own printed design statement reads: "The principal design of this society is to supply destitute congregations... with a succession of able and evangelical ministers," extended, as finances allow, "to encourage evangelical and popular ministers as missionaries and itinerant preachers... where there is an opening for the gospel." Rippon (1795), quoting what appears to be the same design in near-identical words, gives it as: "That dissenting congregations, especially of the Baptist denomination, in any part of the British dominions, may, if it please God, be more effectually supplied with a succession of able and evangelical ministers; and that missionaries may be sent to those places where there is an opening for the gospel." [bba-q001, bba-q008, bba-q009] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847] [PRIMARY: bes-account-1776]
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PO): NOT FOUND in Terrill's own words as quoted this session (the deed excerpts recovered state the purpose but not a stated crisis). Moon (1971) supplies, in his own narrative words rather than a quotation, that the "local context" was the death of Broadmead's minister Thomas Hardcastle after "a long imprisonment," and, more broadly, that after the 1662 Act of Uniformity closed the universities to Nonconformists, Particular Baptist ministers who had previously received a university education (Moon counts "between 40 and 50" before the Code) had no clear route to a learned ministry going forward. [bba-q022, bba-q028] [SECONDARY: moon-1971-caleb-evans]
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): No separate confession or doctrinal-subscription document for the academy or the Society was located this session. Terrill's own condition on his bequest was that the pastor receiving it be "an holy man, well skilled in the tongues, viz. Greek and Hebrew, and doth own and practise the truth of believers' baptism" — a believer's-baptism (Baptist) condition on the tutor, not a wider subscribed confession. [bba-q001, bba-q005] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]
- **F9 Antecedents the founders cite or imitate (earlier schools, documents, persons)** (PO): No source fetched this session records the founders (Terrill, Foskett, or the Evanses) citing an earlier institution or document as their model. Rippon (1795) does record an earlier, apparently unsuccessful, 1675 London Baptist ministers' proposal "to form a plan for... an orderly standing ministry," and a 1689 General Assembly fund for ministerial education, as background developments that preceded Terrill's 1679 deed by four and ten years respectively, but does not present either as something the Bristol founders consciously imitated. [SECONDARY: robinson-1929-250th] NOT FOUND (no direct citation of a model institution by the Bristol founders themselves).

## A — Admission
- **A1 Minimum and maximum age** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: bes-account-1776, rippon-1795-essay, terrill-records-1847, robinson-1929-250th, moon-1971-caleb-evans).
- **A2 Prior education required** (PO): NOT FOUND as a stated entrance requirement. Moon (1971), in his own words, states that "the rules of the Bristol Education Society expressly provides for non-Baptists to share its lectures" — an admission provision for auditors, not a prior-education requirement for the Society's own funded students. [bba-q026] [SECONDARY: moon-1971-caleb-evans]
- **A3 Testimony of conversion, character, or church membership required** (PO): Terrill's deed required students to be "members of any baptized congregation in or about Bristol." The 1770 Society's printed rules similarly state that the Society, "to guard as much as may be against an unconverted ministry," is "determined to receive no students but such as are members of churches, and recommended" — recommendation by their home church stood in place of any exam of personal conversion. The 5 November 1720 admission minute records that the first student, Thomas Rogers, was himself "proposed" and "recommended" by a named church (Mr. Gifford's church, per rippon-1795-essay; the Pithay church, per terrill-records-1847 — see discrepancies.md). [bba-q001, bba-q002, bba-q004, bba-q007] [PRIMARY: bes-account-1776] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]
- **A4 Entrance examination (subjects)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all five fetched documents).
- **A5 Women: admitted? in what capacity?** (PO): NOT FOUND. All named students and tutors in the fetched sources are men; no source fetched this session states a rule excluding women, but none records a woman as student, tutor, or subscriber-member either (the printed rules use "he"/"his" throughout for members and students). [bba-q013] [PRIMARY: bes-account-1776]
- **A6 Prior experience required (e.g., preaching, service)** (PO): NOT FOUND as an explicit rule. Terrill's deed sets a limit on the tutor's time ("three half days in the week" / "three afternoons in the week," per the two quoting sources — see discrepancies.md) and a cap of "not exceeding twelve" students at once, for "two years at the most" — a limit on the course, not a stated prior-experience requirement for entering students. [bba-q002, bba-q005] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]

## C — Curriculum
- **C1 Length of course** (PO): Terrill's deed sets the studentship at "two years at the most." NOT FOUND for the length of the course after the 1770 reorganization under the Evanses. [bba-q002, bba-q020] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847] [SECONDARY: robinson-1929-250th]
- **C2 Subjects, in the order taught** (PO): NOT FOUND as an ordered sequence. Moon (1971), in his own words, states that "Caleb and Hugh introduced their students to languages and logic and rhetoric," and "also included elements of geography, astronomy and natural philosophy," quoting an unattributed period source for the purpose of the latter: "To enlarge and elevate their conceptions of the work of God and His great and glorious perfections." Rippon (1795) records that James Newton, brought in as classical tutor once the Society enlarged the work, was "intimately acquainted" with "the Latin and Greek classics, with the Christian Hebrew scriptures, the Mishnah, Talmuds, and other Jewish writings." [bba-q014, bba-q025] [SECONDARY: moon-1971-caleb-evans] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: rippon-1795-essay]
- **C3 Structure (classes, years, terms)** (PO): NOT FOUND beyond the two-year limit at C1 and the tutor's fixed weekly time commitment at C4/L1.
- **C4 Languages taught, and level required** (PO): Terrill's deed required the tutor to be "well skilled in the tongues, viz. Greek and Hebrew," and to instruct students "in the knowledge of the original languages, and other literature." Newton's later teaching (see C2) extended this to Hebrew scriptural literature (Mishnah, Talmuds). [bba-q001, bba-q005, bba-q014] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]
- **C5 Set texts and authors** (PO): NOT FOUND as a fixed reading list for the academy's own course. A separately published devotional companion recommended by Rippon to the students in his 1795 address (Christiana Militiae Viaticum, extracts from Mather, Brainerd, Edwards, and Owen) is a recommendation appended to Rippon's address, not a set text of the academy's own curriculum, and is not further used here. [SECONDARY: rippon-1795-essay, narrative not independently quoted for this field]
- **C6 Preaching or practical instruction (form, frequency)** (PO): NOT FOUND as a formal exercise or rule this session. Rippon (1795) does record, of Foskett's tutorship in his own words, that "if it be conceded that his method of education was limited rather than liberal, severe rather than enchanting, employing the memory more than the genius, the reasoning more than the softer powers of the mind... it is a piece of justice to his memory... that some good scholars, and several of the greatest ministers... were educated by him" — a characterization of the method's rigor, not a description of a specific practical exercise. [bba-q016] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: rippon-1795-essay]
- **C7 Examinations and their form** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all five fetched documents).
- **C8 Degree, certificate, license, or ordination at completion** (PO): NOT FOUND for the academy's own award. Robinson (1929) records, in his own words, that in 1836 "the College was accepted as an Affiliated College of London University," after which some students could sit London University examinations — but this is a nineteenth-century development, well after this dossier's 1679-1791 period of fetched material, and is only noted here as background. [SECONDARY: robinson-1929-250th]
- **C9 Weekly or daily timetable (table if given)** (PO): NOT FOUND as a timetable. Terrill's deed fixes only the tutor's weekly time commitment: "three half days in the week" per terrill-records-1847, or "three afternoons in the week" per rippon-1795-essay — the two quoting sources differ in this detail; see discrepancies.md. [bba-q002, bba-q005] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]

## L — Common life
- **L1 Daily order: rising, worship, meals, study, bed** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all five fetched documents).
- **L2 Residence: where students lived; with whom** (PO): NOT FOUND. The 1776 Society rules distinguish students who "apply for board as well as education" (who then had to be recommended by a Baptist church) from those receiving "education only" (open to other denominations), implying some students boarded and some did not, but no source fetched this session states where boarding students actually lived. [PRIMARY: bes-account-1776]
- **L3 Worship: form and frequency** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all five fetched documents).
- **L4 Discipline: rules and penalties** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all five fetched documents).
- **L5 Dress, conduct, recreation rules** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all five fetched documents).
- **L6 Fees, board, and how students were supported** (PO): Terrill's deed provided "ten pounds per annum" toward the maintenance of a needy student, "to be applied to the use of any student that might need it, and be approved of by his trustees." The 1770 Society funded students' "board and tuition" from its subscriptions (per the annual accounts headers in bes-account-1776, e.g. "Paid for the Board and Tuition of the Society's Students," not separately quoted as a rule but visible in the printed accounts). Society membership itself required "not less than one guinea" at admission and "one guinea" annual subscription, with £200 or more securing life membership. [bba-q006, bba-q021] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: rippon-1795-essay] [PRIMARY: bes-account-1776]
- **L7 Number of students (with years)** (PS): The bequest capped tutoring at "not exceeding twelve" students at a time. Rippon (1795) records Foskett's own total roll, 1720-1758, as sixty-four students, "thirty-two of them Englishmen, and thirty-two belonging to the Principality" (Wales) — an exact even split he calls "somewhat remarkable." [bba-q002, bba-q012, bba-q013] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]
- **L8 Health, deaths, or hardship recorded among students** (PS): NOT FOUND as a specific record of student deaths or hardship during study. Rippon (1795), in Hugh Evans's own reported words, records only that "most of those who were under Mr. Foskett's care approved themselves truly serious, and with great reputation filled many of our churches" — an outcome report, not a hardship record. [bba-q011] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: rippon-1795-essay]

## T — Teachers
- **T1 Number and titles of teachers (with years)** (PS): Bernard Foskett, tutor/pastor 1720-1758 (38 years, per Robinson 1929). Hugh Evans, tutor/pastor 1758-1781 (per Moon 1971, Caleb succeeded as President in 1779, two years before Hugh's account closes — see discrepancies.md). Caleb Evans, colleague from 1767, President from 1779, died 1791. James Newton, classical tutor from the Society's 1770 enlargement onward. [bba-q014] [SECONDARY: robinson-1929-250th; moon-1971-caleb-evans] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: rippon-1795-essay]
- **T2 Teachers' duties as set out in the charter** (PO): The tutor's duty, per Terrill's deed, was to be "well skilled in the tongues... and devote three half days [or afternoons] in the week to the instruction of any number of young students, not exceeding twelve." [bba-q001, bba-q002, bba-q005] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]
- **T3 Forms of teacher-student contact (lecture, disputation, tutorial, table, correspondence, preaching class)** (PO): NOT FOUND as a named form (e.g. "disputation" or "tutorial") in the fetched sources. Rippon (1795) describes Hugh Evans's manner in his own words as leading "his disciples into the fields of science by a method, in which hourly acquisitions brought new pleasures," and records that Foskett wrote personally to Hugh Evans's father advising the family to "try the young man's ministerial gifts while at home," suggesting individual correspondence and personal assessment of students' gifts as part of the tutor's practice. [bba-q015, bba-q027] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: rippon-1795-essay]
- **T4 Named examples of teacher-student interaction with source** (PS): Bernard Foskett wrote to Hugh Evans's father in Wales, "for while he was at Bristol we could not prevail on him to exercise his gifts, such was his extreme modesty," advising the family to test the young man's ministerial gifts at home — a specific, named instance of a tutor's personal involvement in a student's call to ministry. [bba-q027] [SECONDARY: moon-1971-caleb-evans]
- **T5 Teacher:student ratio if computable (show the two numbers and years)** (PS): One tutor (Foskett) to a cap of twelve students at a time, per Terrill's deed, for the period 1720-1758; against Foskett's total career roll of sixty-four students over 38 years (i.e., a much smaller number in training simultaneously than the cap of twelve implies was often reached, though no source fetched this session states the concurrent enrollment in any given year). [bba-q002, bba-q012] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]

## M — Emphasis
- **M1 What the founders said mattered most (their words)** (PO): The 1770 design statement puts supplying "destitute congregations... with a succession of able and evangelical ministers" first, and sending "missionaries... to those places where there is an opening for the gospel" second, as the funds allowed. Caleb Evans, addressing the students (quoted by Moon from an undated manuscript address, given "three months before" the Society's 1770 founding), asked them to examine their own motives: "Was it merely to have an opportunity of pursuing different branches of literature...? Was it that you might lead an easy, genteel life...? Was it to obtain popular applause and fame...? Or was it from the principle of unfeigned love to Jesus Christ and to the souls of men?" [bba-q008, bba-q009, bba-q024] [PRIMARY: bes-account-1776] [SECONDARY: moon-1971-caleb-evans]
- **M2 Distinctive stress (doctrine, piety, preaching, languages, evangelism, faith principle, self-support, other)** (PO): An educated and converted ministry together, held as inseparable: the 1770 founders' aim, per Moon's own summary, was "two-fold[:] to put the work on a sound financial footing, and to make possible a more liberal type of education," on the conviction that "there was no dichotomy between the devotional and the academic." Rippon (1795) records that even a critic of Foskett's severe method conceded good scholars and "several of the greatest ministers" came from it. [bba-q016, bba-q023] [SECONDARY: moon-1971-caleb-evans] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: rippon-1795-essay]
- **M3 What the founders explicitly rejected or warned against** (PO): The Society's rules state its purpose "to guard as much as may be against an unconverted ministry, which, whether learned or unlearned, is the bane of religion" — an explicit rejection of learning divorced from conversion, in either direction. [bba-q001] [PRIMARY: bes-account-1776]
- **M4 Motto or watchword, with earliest source** (PS): NOT FOUND as a fixed motto for this period. (The roster records no motto for this institution.)
- **M5 Later leaders' restatements of the emphasis (with dates)** (PS): NOT FOUND within this dossier's fetched period (1679-1791); Robinson (1929) restates the institution's value in his own words at its 250th anniversary but that is outside the book's founding-document focus and is not further used here.

## S — Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)** (PO): No separate sending body distinct from church commendation is recorded for this period; students were admitted on recommendation by their home churches (see A3), and — per Robinson (1929), in his own words — among the "thirteen who met in 1792 and formed" the Baptist Missionary Society at Kettering were "four former [Bristol] students, Dr. John Ryland, John Sutcliffe, Samuel Pearce and Thomas Blundell," plus a then-current Bristol student (later Dr. William Staughton) who "borrowed the half-guinea which he contributed to that first collection." [bba-q017] [SECONDARY: robinson-1929-250th]
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): The 1770 design statement speaks generally of "those places where there is an opening for the gospel" rather than naming destinations; Robinson (1929) records that Bristol-trained men went on to found or lead sister colleges "at home or abroad," naming Rhode Island College (later Brown University, founded by Morgan Edwards, a Foskett student) as the "earliest daughter-college," planted in America. [bba-q008, bba-q017] [PRIMARY: bes-account-1776] [SECONDARY: robinson-1929-250th]
- **S3 Numbers sent, by period, with source** (PS): Robinson (1929), writing in his own words at the College's 250th anniversary (1929) and looking back over its whole history to that date rather than only this dossier's 1679-1791 period, states that "more than a hundred Bristol students have become missionaries" and, separately, that "forty-two Bristol students have become Principals or tutors of colleges at home or abroad" — both cumulative totals as of 1929, not figures for the 1679-1791 founding period specifically. [bba-q018, bba-q019] [SECONDARY: robinson-1929-250th]
- **S4 Support on the field (salary, faith principle, self-support)** (PO): NOT FOUND for missionaries specifically; for students in training, see L6 (bequest maintenance and Society-funded board/tuition).
- **S5 Deaths, martyrdoms, or casualties recorded, with source** (PS): NOT FOUND (searched: all five fetched documents).
- **S6 Instructions given to those sent (title, date, document_id)** (PO): NOT FOUND as a separate instructions document for missionaries. The closest analogue in the fetched material is the trustees' own admission minute of 5 November 1720, which functions as an instruction for admitting (not sending) a student: "Thomas Rogers... was proposed as a person suitable for a student under the direction of brother Foskett, according to Mr. Terrill's deed... Agreed he should have the liberty of learning." [bba-q004, bba-q007] [PRIMARY-ADJACENT: terrill-records-1847, rippon-1795-essay]
