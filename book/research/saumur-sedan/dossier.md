# Dossier — Saumur/Sedan (`saumur-sedan`)

Note on sourcing: no manuscript primary source (council register, letters patent, printed
statutes) was independently located and read this session. All facts and quotations below come
from three peer-reviewed academic chapters (Didier Boisson and Jean-Paul Pittion on Saumur;
Aurelien Behr on Sedan), published open access by OpenEdition Books/LARHRA in *Protestantisme et
education dans la France moderne* (2018), which quote verbatim, with folio- and page-level
footnotes, from the academies' own manuscript council registers, period letters, and the 1620
Synod of Ales' printed General Statutes for the Reformed Academies of France — plus three English
pages from the Musee protestant (Virtual Museum of Protestantism) and two Wikipedia articles used
for background/orientation. Passages the LARHRA chapters themselves quote in guillemets from a
named archival source are cited PO (treated as primary, following the same convention used for
Borgeaud in `geneva-academy`); the chapters' own narrative sentences are cited PS. This entry
covers two institutions together, as the roster instructs: the Academy of Saumur (college from
1593, academy from c. 1599-1606) and the Academy of Sedan (college from 1577/78, academy from
1601/1602), both closed in the 1680s.

## F — Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): Saumur: a college (from 1593) and, as a distinct body, an academy for theological formation of pastors (from c. 1599-1606); together often called "le college et l'academie de Saumur." Sedan: a college (from 1577/78) and, later, an academy (from 1601/1602). [ss-q001, ss-q017, ss-q018, ss-q019]
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): Saumur — letters patent March 1593 authorizing the college; academy register-keeping from 1613; the academy's own functioning dated by Boisson to "à partir de 1606"; the Musee protestant page instead gives "1599-1600." Sedan — college functioning attested end of 1577/start of 1578; higher courses given without forming an academy from 1573; the academy's functioning attested only in 1602, with Henri de La Tour d'Auvergne named official founder; the Musee protestant page instead credits the 1601 National Synod of Gergeau with the upgrade. See discrepancies.md items 1-3 for the several dates given by different sources and not reconciled here. [ss-q001, ss-q017, ss-q018, ss-q019, ss-q030, ss-q031, ss-q033, ss-q040, ss-q041]
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PS): Saumur — the college and academy's founding is credited to Philippe Duplessis-Mornay (1549-1623), governor of Saumur from 1588, who obtained the 1593 letters patent from Henri IV and remained a governing presence at the academic council into the early 1620s. Sedan — the college's founding is credited to Francoise de Bourbon, regent after her husband's death, who acted on his wishes; the academy's official founder is named as Henri de La Tour d'Auvergne, prince of Sedan from 1591. Toussaint Berchet was the college's first principal (1577-1605), teaching eloquence and rhetoric. [ss-q001, ss-q008, ss-q018, ss-q030, ss-q040]
- **F4 Place(s)** (PS): Saumur, on the Loire. Sedan, in the sovereign principality of Sedan (attached to France only in 1642). Unlike Saumur, Montauban, and other academies, Sedan's academy had no building of its own: it was, in Behr's words, "not a building but a body of professors" and students, with courses given in various places — the town hall, rooms of the jeu de paume (real-tennis court), teachers' own homes, or the college. [ss-q020, ss-q021]
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): No single charter document was located and read this session. The closest equivalents identified: the March 1593 letters patent for Saumur's college (quoted at second hand, document_id `larhra-boisson-saumur`); the "statuts generaux pour les Academies des Eglises reformees de France," adopted at the National Synod of Ales, 1620, which governed Saumur, Sedan, and all other Reformed academies alike and is quoted at length by Boisson from the Saumur council register (document_id `larhra-boisson-saumur`) and summarized by the Musee protestant (document_id `museeprotestant-reformed-academies`). [ss-q001, ss-q003, ss-q034]
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): The 1593 letters patent authorize Duplessis-Mornay "faire construire, eriger et batir un college" ("to build, erect and construct a college") at Saumur — a phrase about the college, not explicitly about ministerial training, though the academy that followed was, per every secondary source read, for theological formation of pastors. [ss-q001]
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PS): NOT FOUND as a directly quoted founder's statement of crisis or need this session; both academies are described by every source as existing to supply trained Reformed ministers to the French Reformed churches, a purpose stated generally (see M1-M2) rather than tied to a specific quoted crisis narrative.
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): The masters and teachers of the Reformed colleges and academies "all had to sign the confession of faith and the church code of the Reformed Churches," a decision taken at the 1620 National Synod of Ales. The same synod's General Statutes, as transcribed into the Saumur register, specifically restrict who may teach Greek: "a minister may be professor of theology and of the Hebrew language, but it is not fitting that he practice the profession of the Greek language," because Greek study chiefly serves the exposition of "pagan and profane authors." [ss-q035, ss-q012]
- **F9 Antecedents the founders cite or imitate (earlier schools, documents, persons)** (PS): "As early as 1565, the synods of the Reformed Churches undertook the training of pastors, encouraging churches to open colleges... and universities or 'academies' (after the model of the academy founded by Calvin in Geneva in 1559)." Sedan's college is also stated to have been inspired by "les structures sedanaise et orthezienne" being cited in turn by Saumur's own founders to justify their own college — i.e., Sedan and Orthez's academies are cited by Saumur's founders as precedent. [ss-q029, text/larhra-behr-sedan.txt paragraph 1 — the Sedan/Orthez-cited-by-Saumur sentence is present in the fetched text but not independently formalized as a separate quote_id this session; see follow-up note]

## A — Admission
- **A1 Minimum and maximum age** (PO): No fixed minimum age is stated in any source read this session. Individual examples: at Saumur, Philippe Le Noir joined the college's third class in 1638 at age 15 (with other classmates of similar age), left the top class after two years, and returned at 20 to finish his master's-level studies. At Sedan, Pierre Bayle wrote in a September 1676 letter that his own pupils were "almost all between 16 and 18 years old." [ss-q016, ss-q024]
- **A2 Prior education required** (PO): NOT FOUND as a stated formal requirement this session; the youngest college pupils are described as arriving already able to read (taught at home by a mother, grandmother, or tutor, or at local "petites ecoles"), implying basic literacy was expected before college entry, though this is a general social description, not an admission rule.
- **A3 Testimony of conversion, character, or church membership required** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all eight documents this session).
- **A4 Entrance examination (subjects)** (PO): NOT FOUND as a specific entrance-exam description; annual/periodic examination of students generally by two pastors deputed yearly by the provincial synod is attested (see C7). Pierre Bayle's 1676 letter is also quoted describing his own theology students, disparagingly, as not being "an audience of great penetration" — a personal assessment of quality, not a formal examination record. [ss-q024b, ss-q007]
- **A5 Women: admitted? in what capacity?** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all eight documents this session) as students; women appear in the sources only as founders (Francoise de Bourbon, regent, credited with Sedan's college) or as family members housing students.
- **A6 Prior experience required (e.g., preaching, service)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all eight documents this session).

## C — Curriculum
- **C1 Length of course** (PO): "After a two years course consisting mainly of philosophy and leading to a Master of Arts degree, the student could go on for a three years course in theology" — a five-year total structure stated for the Reformed academies generally, not tied to Saumur or Sedan specifically in this source. [ss-q036]
- **C2 Subjects, in the order taught** (PO): The 1620 Ales General Statutes required each academy to provide courses in theology (one or two professors of the Holy Scriptures, one of Doctrine), Hebrew, Greek, Philosophy, Rhetoric, and Mathematics. At its founding period, Saumur's academy had two Chairs of Theology and one each in Hebrew, Greek, and (two) Philosophy. Sedan's early higher-level teachers (from 1573, before the academy proper) covered chronology, Hebrew, philosophy, and later law and physics. [ss-q034, ss-q033]
- **C3 Structure (classes, years, terms)** (PO): At Saumur, the college and the academy were formally two bodies — the college with its regents under a principal, the academy with its professors under a rector — though in practice closely linked; the 1620 General Statutes fix two governing councils for each academy, "one ordinary," composed of local pastors and public professors together with the college's first regent, headed by the rector, "the other extraordinary [...]." Within the college, each year of study corresponded to a class, pupils grouped in sections of ten ("decuries"), each with a "superintendent" chosen by the regent by order of enrollment. [ss-q003, ss-q015]
- **C4 Languages taught, and level required** (PO): Latin, Greek, and Hebrew are all attested as taught (see C2); catechism instruction was given "in French for the lowest classes, and in Latin for the highest, the first and second," per the academy's own register. A minister could teach Hebrew but, per the 1620 Statutes as transcribed into the Saumur register, was discouraged from teaching Greek specifically because Greek study served the exposition of "pagan and profane authors" (see F8). [ss-q011, ss-q012]
- **C5 Set texts and authors** (PO): NOT FOUND as a specific reading list this session; "pagan and profane authors" are referenced generically in connection with Greek instruction (see F8/C4), without titles named.
- **C6 Preaching or practical instruction (form, frequency)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: all eight documents this session) as a described exercise; students are, however, repeatedly recorded attending the Sunday sermon (le preche) as a body, seated by class rank (see L3).
- **C7 Examinations and their form** (PO): Provincial synods where an academy existed were required to depute two pastors annually, from outside the academy's own churches, to travel to the academy "to examine all the students, and to see whether the professors are doing their duty" — a provision from the 1617 Synod of Vitre, which also insisted "in a very express manner" on strict observance of the academic statutes generally. [ss-q006, ss-q007]
- **C8 Degree, certificate, license, or ordination at completion** (PO): A Master of Arts degree followed the two-year philosophy course, before the three-year theology course (see C1); NOT FOUND for a specific theology-completion degree or license procedure this session, though the great majority of theology graduates are recorded (at Sedan) as becoming ministers (see S3). [ss-q036]
- **C9 Weekly or daily timetable (table if given)** (PO): NOT FOUND as an hour-by-hour timetable this session; the fixed weekly points recorded are: catechism on Sunday and Wednesday mornings, and attendance at the Sunday and Saturday sermon (le preche), from which the regents' duty of conducting their pupils is separately noted. [ss-q011]

## L — Common life
- **L1 Daily order: rising, worship, meals, study, bed** (PO): NOT FOUND as a full daily order this session; catechism on Sunday/Wednesday mornings and preaching attendance (see C9) are the only fixed recurring points located.
- **L2 Residence: where students lived; with whom** (PO): Neither academy had its own dormitory/pensionnat for the college; students boarded with private householders of the local Reformed church, including sometimes with the academy's own professors or the college's regents (which supplemented their low wages). At Saumur, the 1614 council forbade enrolling or examining a new student until he was lodged with the rector's, principal's, or a pastor's knowledge and consent, and forbade changing lodgings without the same consent, "which shall be made known to the parents." At Sedan, with no dedicated building at all, "the academy is not a building but a body of professors" and students, taught at the town hall, the jeu de paume rooms, teachers' own houses, or the college. [ss-q005, ss-q020, ss-q021]
- **L3 Worship: form and frequency** (PO): Regents brought their pupils to the sermon (le preche) on Wednesdays and Saturdays and drilled them beforehand, in class, on the catechism, held itself on Sunday and Wednesday mornings, in French for the lower classes and Latin for the two highest. [ss-q011]
- **L4 Discipline: rules and penalties** (PO): Saumur's regents swore an oath of obedience to the college principal "concerning both the manner of teaching and the discipline and punishment of the students." The 1614 council rule against unauthorized change of lodging was explicitly aimed at students who "flee the lodgings of the regents or of other authoritative persons who control their actions and keep them in check," preferring lodgings with people who "let them do as they please and foster their debauchery." Duelling or bearing arms among students brought whipping "without remission," with "the full rigor of discipline possible" applied to philosophy and theology students generally for lesser involvement. [ss-q004, ss-q014, ss-q009]
- **L5 Dress, conduct, recreation rules** (PO): A 1665 provincial synod at Saumur recorded complaints that theology students were "too showily dressed," given to "several forms of debauchery," neglecting study "to frequent girls" whom they walked publicly through the streets. [ss-q010]
- **L6 Fees, board, and how students were supported** (PO): Saumur's academic council fixed a boarding-fee schedule as early as 1619, with three tiers ("tables") of 100, 120, and 150 livres for an 11-month school year, rising steadily over the century; by 1633 provincial synods complained that only "seigneurs and others of great means" could still afford to send sons to study at Saumur. [ss-q013]
- **L7 Number of students (with years)** (PS): At Sedan, a published catalogue of theses defended 1608-1660 (printed at Geneva, 1661) names 93 students with geographic origin; cross-referenced against the Haag brothers' broader records, 221 names in total are recoverable, of whom nearly three-quarters (168) were of French or Sedanais origin. [text/larhra-behr-sedan.txt, "Une communaute cosmopolite" section — summarized from the fetched text; the 93/221 figures were read but not independently formalized as a separate quote_id this session]
- **L8 Health, deaths, or hardship recorded among students** (PS): One student death is recorded at Saumur: a logic student named Dupas died in 1648 following a "batterie" between some students and academicians the previous evening — NOT FOUND as an independently formalized quote this session, though the fact appears in the fetched larhra-boisson-saumur.txt text.

## T — Teachers
- **T1 Number and titles of teachers (with years)** (PS): Saumur distinguished, by title, the college's regents (under a principal) from the academy's public professors (under a rector); the 1620 General Statutes fix the academic council's composition as pastors and public professors plus the college's first regent, under the rector. Sedan recorded sixty identifiable professors across its history, twenty of foreign origin (eight of them Scots) and around twenty-three of French but non-Sedanais origin, plus eleven born in Sedan itself. [ss-q003, text/larhra-behr-sedan.txt "Les origines des professeurs" section]
- **T2 Teachers' duties as set out in the charter** (PO): Regents swore obedience to the principal "concerning both the manner of teaching and the discipline and punishment of the students"; provincial synods deputed two outside pastors annually to check that "the professors are doing their duty." [ss-q004, ss-q007]
- **T3 Forms of teacher–student contact (lecture, disputation, tutorial, table, correspondence, preaching class)** (PO): Lecture and catechetical drilling by regents; annual/periodic external examination by deputed pastors; direct governance contact through the academic council, where the rector personally intervened in disputes — as when Duplessis-Mornay, present as governor at the 19 April 1615 council, heard two candidates for a regent's post, one of whom complained that the governor's "presence had troubled him and that he had hoped to speak more boldly outside his presence." [ss-q007, ss-q008]
- **T4 Named examples of teacher–student interaction with source** (PS): The 19 April 1615 council incident above (Duplessis-Mornay and the two regent-candidates) is the clearest named example. [ss-q008]
- **T5 Teacher:student ratio if computable (show the two numbers and years)** (PS): NOT FOUND as a computable ratio this session.

## M — Emphasis
- **M1 What the founders said mattered most (their words)** (PO): "A student thus acquired a sound basis in theology, necessary to a 'minister of the word of God,' whose 'main duty was to preach.'" "The primary aim of the Reformed Churches was to train future ministers." [ss-q037, ss-q039]
- **M2 Distinctive stress (doctrine, piety, preaching, languages, evangelism, faith principle, self-support, other)** (PO): Preparation to preach, grounded in theology and the biblical languages, was the stated primary aim, though "some academies, for instance those of Orthez and Sedan, also offered courses in other subjects, such as law or medicine." A historian is quoted (at second hand, by Behr) describing the wider character of these academies: "the Protestant universities are international in their professors as in their students," a claim Sedan's own recruitment record and Duplessis-Mornay's 1607 remark that at Sedan "the students flock there from every direction" are both offered to support. [ss-q037, ss-q022, ss-q023]
- **M3 What the founders explicitly rejected or warned against** (PO): Idleness, drink and debauchery in lodging chosen to escape supervision (L4); showy dress, "debauchery," and neglect of study for the company of girls (L5); duelling and the bearing of arms among students (L4). [ss-q014, ss-q010, ss-q009]
- **M4 Motto or watchword, with earliest source** (PS): NOT FOUND (searched: all eight documents this session).
- **M5 Later leaders' restatements of the emphasis (with dates)** (PS): NOT FOUND (searched: all eight documents this session).

## S — Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)** (PO): No single sending ceremony or licensing clause was located and quoted this session. Provincial and national synods financed, supervised, and (via deputed pastors) examined the academies (see C7); graduates who became ministers are recorded as taking up posts in French Reformed churches generally, with the crown itself (Henri IV and Louis XIII) granting funds to Sedan's academy in part because most of its ministers went on to serve within the kingdom. [ss-q007, text/larhra-behr-sedan.txt paragraph on royal funding — read but not independently formalized as a separate quote_id]
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): At Sedan, nearly two-thirds of traceable students returned to their home regions; Normandy was the most common destination for Sedan-trained ministers (26 traced), followed by the Ile-de-France (15) and Champagne (11). A minority (about twenty) took up ministry abroad, mostly in the United Provinces and Switzerland. [ss-q028]
- **S3 Numbers sent, by period, with source** (PS): Of 221 traceable Sedan students, the careers of 80% could be reconstructed; "a little more than three-quarters became ministers." [ss-q027, ss-q028]
- **S4 Support on the field (salary, faith principle, self-support)** (PO): The French crown funded Sedan's academy until the 1631 Synod of Charenton, after which "the King of France no longer paid funds to the Academy of Sedan." NOT FOUND for individual ministers' field support once placed. [ss-q025]
- **S5 Deaths, martyrdoms, or casualties recorded, with source** (PS): One student death at Saumur (Dupas, 1648, after a "batterie" between students) is present in the fetched text but not independently formalized as a quote this session (see L8). NOT FOUND for any death connected to a graduate's ministry or sending specifically.

## R — Fruit
- **R1 Pierre Bayle (1647-1706)** (PS): Student, then from 1675 professor of philosophy, at Sedan; left at the academy's 1681 closure; later a leading figure associated with the coming Enlightenment. [ss-q026, ss-q024]
- **R2 Pierre Jurieu (1637-1713)** (PS): Professor of theology (and Hebrew) at Sedan from 1673 to the 1681 closure, when he too left; grandson of Pierre Du Moulin; became a pastor of the Huguenot Refuge and a defender of resistance to Louis XIV. [ss-q026]
- **R3 Pierre Du Moulin** (PS): Theology professor at Sedan (1621-1658), a refugee there from prosecution within the kingdom of France, who stayed despite "multiple outside temptations" to leave. [SECONDARY: larhra-behr-sedan, paragraph on career-long Sedan residents — read but not independently formalized as a quote this session]
- **R4 Philippe Duplessis-Mornay (1549-1623)** (PS): Governor of Saumur from 1588; obtained the 1593 letters patent founding its college and is credited across every source read with founding its academy (on dates ranging 1599-1606, see discrepancies.md item 1); remained an active presence at its academic council into the 1620s. [ss-q001, ss-q008, ss-q040, ss-q041]
