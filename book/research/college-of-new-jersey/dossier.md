# Dossier — College of New Jersey (`college-of-new-jersey`)

Note on sourcing: one volume was fetched and read in full this session — Maclean 1877, Vol. I,
the standard institutional history, written by the College's tenth President, covering 1746
through 1854 (this dossier draws only on the founding period, 1746-1766, i.e. the charters and
the administrations of Presidents Dickinson, Burr, Edwards, Davies, and Finley). Maclean's own
connecting narrative is secondary; but he quotes at length, and clearly marked off by quotation
marks, from primary records he had before him: the Trustees' minute book, the full text of the
1748 Charter, contemporary letters (Governor Belcher, the Synod of New York), and — most
valuably for curriculum and daily life — the 1764 pamphlet "An Account of the College of New
Jersey," an official account written (per Maclean's own account of its authorship) by Samuel
Blair and published by order of the Trustees. Passages so quoted are treated as PRIMARY here
and cited to their originating document (the 1748 Charter, the 1764 Account, a named letter),
with document_id `maclean1877-v1` since that is the fetched text. Volume II (Witherspoon
onward, from 1768) was not fetched this session; per the roster note, Witherspoon is a later
president and outside this chapter's founding-period scope. The text of the 1746 (first)
charter does not survive in any known copy — Maclean states this explicitly (cnj-q007) — so the
abridged charter in this chapter is the 1748 (second) charter only, which does survive in full
and is quoted whole by Maclean.

## F — Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): The 1748 Charter incorporates
  the institution as "THE TRUSTEES OF THE COLLEGE OF NEW JERSEY," its sole corporate name.
  [cnj-q011] The first College building, completed 1756, was named "Nassau Hall" at Governor
  Belcher's own request, and the College came to be popularly called by this name as well.
  [cnj-q020] Its later renaming as Princeton University (1896) is well known but was not
  independently confirmed against a source read this session; not stated as a chapter fact.
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): The first charter
  passed the great seal of New Jersey on 22 October 1746, granted by John Hamilton, President of
  the Council and acting Governor. [cnj-q003] Its own text does not survive. [cnj-q007] The
  College opened for instruction at Elizabethtown in the fourth week of May 1747, under
  President Jonathan Dickinson. [cnj-q017, cnj-q018] Dickinson died on 7 October 1747, within
  five months of the opening. [cnj-q019] A second charter, sought to remove doubts about
  Hamilton's authority to grant the first, was given by Governor Jonathan Belcher and passed the
  great seal on 14 September 1748. [cnj-q008]
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PS): The first charter (1746) was granted
  to Jonathan Dickinson, John Pierson, Ebenezer Pemberton, and Aaron Burr, "ministers of the
  gospel, and some other gentlemen," as Trustees. [cnj-q006] Dickinson was appointed and served
  as the College's first President. [cnj-q017] Aaron Burr (Sr.) succeeded Dickinson as President
  under the first charter and continued as the first President named in the second (1748)
  charter. [cnj-q006, cnj-q008 context in dossier discussion below]
- **F4 Place(s)** (PO): Elizabethtown (opening, 1747). [cnj-q017, cnj-q018] The first
  Commencement, delayed by the preparation of the second charter, was finally held at Newark on
  9 November 1748. [cnj-q046] The College's permanent home from 1756 was Princeton, in the
  building named Nassau Hall. [cnj-q020]
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): The Charter of
  the College of New Jersey, given by Governor Jonathan Belcher, passed the great seal of the
  Province of New Jersey on 14 September 1748, in English; document_id `maclean1877-v1` (Chapter
  III reprints the charter in full). The 1746 first charter's text does not survive (see F2);
  the 1748 charter is therefore the document abridged in this chapter's Charter section.
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): "The chief aim of the founders and early
  friends of the College was to furnish the Church, and more especially their own branch of it,
  with a pious and learned ministry." [cnj-q001] The Charter's own preamble recites "the great
  necessity of coming into some method for encouraging and promoting a learned education of our
  youth in New Jersey," founding the College "for the education of youth in the learned
  languages and in the liberal arts and sciences." [cnj-q002] The parties who received the
  (lost) first charter described it as "a charter with full and ample privileges," under which
  "equal liberties and privileges are secured to every denomination of Christians, any different
  religious sentiments notwithstanding." [cnj-q004, cnj-q005]
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PO): The College's origin is tied
  by Maclean directly to the first schism in the American Presbyterian Church, beginning in 1741
  with the separation of the Presbytery of New Brunswick from the Synod of Philadelphia.
  [cnj-q014] After the schism, ministers of the Presbytery of New York — unable to unite with
  the Synod of Philadelphia's own school and "not satisfied with the limited course of
  instruction given at the Neshaminy school" (the Log College) — "turned their thoughts [to] the
  erecting of a college, in which ample provision should be made for the intellectual and
  religious culture of youth desirous to obtain a liberal education, and more especially for the
  thorough training of such as were candidates for the holy ministry." [cnj-q016]
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): The Charter itself imposes
  no doctrinal test on students: its preamble recites the founders' "earnest desire that those
  of every religious denomination may have free and equal liberty and advantages of education in
  the said college, any different sentiments in religion notwithstanding," [cnj-q009] and its
  operative clause on the Trustees' law-making power binds them to make no law "excluding any
  person of any religious de-nomination whatsoever from free and equal liberty and advantage of
  education... on account of his or their being of a religious profession different from the
  said trustees." [cnj-q010] The Charter does require the Trustees themselves, on first meeting,
  to take specified oaths against Roman Catholicism and for the Hanoverian succession (cut from
  the abridged charter as administrative detail; not quoted here as a distinct record). No
  requirement of doctrinal subscription by students, or a specific confession bound on them, was
  found in the material read this session; contrast this with Andover's or Princeton
  Theological Seminary's later subscription requirements, which the College of New Jersey's own
  1748 charter does not impose.
- **F9 Antecedents the founders cite or imitate (earlier schools, documents, persons)** (PO): The
  founders' own antecedent, per Maclean, was the Log College, William Tennent Sr.'s school on
  the Neshaminy in Pennsylvania, where several New Brunswick-party ministers had trained; the
  College of New Jersey's founders (from the rival New York Presbytery) judged the Log College's
  "comparatively meagre instruction in the arts and sciences" and its emphasis on "personal piety
  and religious experience, in candidates for the ministry" over "a complete knowledge of both
  their preparatory and their professional studies" as insufficient, and sought something of a
  "higher order." [cnj-q015] The Synod of Philadelphia had itself, in 1739, unanimously approved
  a plan to erect "a school or seminary of learning" and to send agents to Europe to raise funds
  for it — a plan abandoned after war broke out between England and Spain in 1740, before the
  1741 schism; this Synod-wide project is recorded by Maclean but no quote record was made of it
  this session (not directly claimed by the College's own founders as their model). NOT FOUND
  (searched: maclean1877-v1): any passage in which the founders themselves cite Harvard, Yale, or
  a European university by name as a model followed.

## A — Admission
- **A1 Minimum and maximum age** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: maclean1877-v1).
- **A2 Prior education required** (PO): The first College laws, adopted under President Burr in
  1748, required candidates to be examined by the President and Tutors and "found able to render
  Virgil and Tully's Orations into English; and to turn English into true and grammatical Latin;
  and to be so well acquainted with the Greek as to render any part of the four Evangelists in
  that language into Latin or English; and to give the grammatical connexion of the words."
  [cnj-q025] By 1764, the published Account states candidates for the Freshman class "must be
  capable of composing grammatical Latin, translating Virgil, Cicero's Orations, and the four
  Evangelists in Greek; and by a late order [made in Mr. Davies's administration] must understand
  the principal rules of vulgar arithmetic." [cnj-q034]
- **A3 Testimony of conversion, character, or church membership required** (PO): No requirement
  of a personal conversion narrative or church-membership testimony for admission was found in
  the material read this session; the Charter's own clauses on religious liberty (see F8) instead
  bar any exclusion "on account of his or their being of a religious profession different from
  the said trustees." [cnj-q009, cnj-q010] NOT FOUND (searched: maclean1877-v1): any distinct
  admission-testimony requirement.
- **A4 Entrance examination (subjects)** (PO): Every entering student was examined by the
  President and Tutors in Latin translation and composition and in Greek (the four Gospels).
  [cnj-q025, cnj-q034] Candidates for classes above the Freshman year were "not only previously
  examined, but recite a fortnight upon trial, in that particular class for which they offer
  themselves; and are then fixed in that, or a lower, as they happen to be judged qualified. But,
  unless in very singular and extraordinary cases, none are received after the Junior year."
  [cnj-q035]
- **A5 Women: admitted? in what capacity?** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: maclean1877-v1). No
  mention of women students, in any capacity, was located in the material read this session.
- **A6 Prior experience required (e.g., preaching, service)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched:
  maclean1877-v1).

## C — Curriculum
- **C1 Length of course** (PO): Four years, divided into the Freshman, Sophomore, Junior, and
  Senior classes, "in each of these they continue one year." [cnj-q027, cnj-q028]
- **C2 Subjects, in the order taught** (PO): "As to the branches of literature taught here, they
  are the same with those which are made parts of education in the European Colleges."
  [cnj-q027] The Freshman year was "spent in Latin and Greek languages, particularly in reading
  Horace, Cicero's Orations, the Greek Testament, Lucian's Dialogues, and Xenophon's
  Cyropaedia." [cnj-q029] The Sophomore year continued the languages (Homer, Longinus) and added
  geography, rhetoric, logic, and mathematics (per the surrounding, unquoted narrative of the
  1764 Account as summarized by Maclean). The Junior year continued mathematics and added
  natural and moral philosophy, metaphysics, and chronology, "and the greater number, especially
  such as are educating for the service of the church, are initiated into the Hebrew."
  [cnj-q030] "The Senior year is entirely employed in reviews and composition," revising Latin
  and Greek classics, part of the Hebrew Bible, and all the arts and sciences studied. [cnj-q030]
- **C3 Structure (classes, years, terms)** (PO): Four classes — Freshman, Sophomore, Junior,
  Senior — "giving and receiving in their turns those tokens of respect and subjection which
  belong to their standings, in order to preserve a due subordination." [cnj-q028]
- **C4 Languages taught, and level required** (PO): Latin and Greek from entrance (required for
  admission itself, see A2); Hebrew introduced in the Junior year, especially for those preparing
  for the ministry. [cnj-q025, cnj-q030]
- **C5 Set texts and authors** (PO): Freshman year: Horace, Cicero's Orations, the Greek
  Testament, Lucian's Dialogues, Xenophon's Cyropaedia. [cnj-q029] A student's own 1750 letters
  (Joseph Shippen, quoted by Maclean) additionally name Xenophon, Watts' "Ontology," Cicero de
  Oratore, the Hebrew Grammar, Tully's Orations, Virgil, and Watts' "Logick" as texts studied.
  [cnj-q045]
- **C6 Preaching or practical instruction (form, frequency)** (PO): "The weekly course of
  disputation is continued... They discuss two or three theses in a week; some in the syllogistic
  and others in the forensic manner, alternately," the forensic disputations conducted in
  English. [cnj-q031] NOT FOUND this session for a distinct sermon-preparation or preaching class
  (as opposed to disputation) among the primary quotations gathered.
- **C7 Examinations and their form** (PO): Besides admission examinations, "the last examination
  of the Senior class, previous to their obtaining the first collegiate honors" and annual
  examinations of the three lower classes were held, "in order for admission into the next," with
  students found unqualified "not allowed to rise in the usual course." [cnj-q035, and see
  surrounding unquoted narrative]
- **C8 Degree, certificate, license, or ordination at completion** (PO): The Charter empowers the
  Trustees, "for the encouragement of learning and animating the students of the said college to
  diligence, industry, and a laudable progress in literature," to "give and grant any such degree
  and degrees to any of the students of the said college... as are usually granted in either of
  our universities or any other college in our realm of Great Britain," with diplomas or
  certificates sealed and kept by the graduates. [cnj-q012, cnj-q013] No separate license or
  ordination was conferred by the College itself; ordination and licensure for the ministry
  proceeded afterward through Presbytery, not through the College (inferred from the absence of
  any College licensing clause in the material read; not directly quoted).
- **C9 Weekly or daily timetable (table if given)** (PO): "Each class recites twice a day: and
  have always free access to their teachers, to solve any difficulties that may occur. The bell
  rings for morning prayer at six o'clock, when the Senior class read off a chapter from the
  original into English." [cnj-q032] A student's own 1750 account of the Freshman-year routine:
  recitation to the President at seven each morning in Xenophon (Greek) and Watts' "Ontology";
  the rest of the morning spent on Cicero de Oratore and Hebrew grammar, recited to the Tutor;
  the remainder of the day spent restudying the same for the next morning's recitation; one
  weekly syllogistic disputation. [cnj-q045]

## L — Common life
- **L1 Daily order: rising, worship, meals, study, bed** (PO): Morning prayer at six o'clock, at
  which the Senior class read a chapter of Scripture from the original language and the
  president questioned them on it. [cnj-q032] A student's 1750 letters describe a day filled
  from seven in the morning with fixed recitations (see C9). [cnj-q045] NOT FOUND (searched:
  maclean1877-v1) for a specific bedtime or the exact hour of meals in the founding period
  itself (later, 1781-era rules on lodging and board were found but are outside this chapter's
  founding-period scope and not quoted here).
- **L2 Residence: where students lived; with whom** (PO): The Nassau Hall building, completed
  1756, had sixty rooms in all (exclusive of the Chapel), of which forty-nine "were assigned to
  the lodging of students, and... deemed sufficient for one hundred and forty-seven, reckoning
  three to a chamber." [cnj-q038]
- **L3 Worship: form and frequency** (PO): Daily morning prayer, with Scripture reading and
  examination by the president. [cnj-q032] NOT FOUND (searched: maclean1877-v1) for evening
  worship or a separate Sabbath-service description within the founding-period material quoted
  this session (a Sabbath-preaching arrangement is described later, in the Princeton period, but
  was not quoted as a founding-era primary record).
- **L4 Discipline: rules and penalties** (PO): Every entering student was required to
  "transcribe the Laws, which being signed by the President, shall be testimony of his
  admission, and shall be kept by him, while he remains a member of the College, as the rule of
  his Behavior." [cnj-q026] By 1764, of 120 students there were "very few whose conduct rendered
  them obnoxious even to the milder methods of punishment"; the laws authorized fines, though by
  this period admonition and suspension had become the ordinary penalties, with expulsion
  reserved to the Trustees. [cnj-q036, and unquoted surrounding narrative for the expulsion
  detail]
- **L5 Dress, conduct, recreation rules** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: maclean1877-v1) within the
  founding-period material quoted this session.
- **L6 Fees, board, and how students were supported** (PO): NOT FOUND as a founding-period (pre-
  1766) figure among the quotations gathered this session; a detailed fee schedule survives from
  1794 (Witherspoon's presidency) but falls outside this chapter's scope and was not quoted.
- **L7 Number of students (with years)** (PS): 120 students in 1764. [cnj-q036] The College
  building was designed to house 147 students, three to a chamber, as of its 1756 completion.
  [cnj-q038] 130 total graduates during Samuel Finley's presidency (1761-1766) are recorded by
  name. [cnj-q039]
- **L8 Health, deaths, or hardship recorded among students** (PS): President Dickinson himself
  died within five months of the College's opening, on 7 October 1747. [cnj-q019] No student
  deaths or hardships were found and quoted this session; NOT FOUND (searched: maclean1877-v1).

## T — Teachers
- **T1 Number and titles of teachers (with years)** (PS): "From the beginning, the government and

- **T2 Teachers' duties as set out in the charter** (PO): The Charter empowers the Trustees to

  professors, to assist the president... in the education and government of the students" as

## M — Emphasis
- **M1 What the founders said mattered most (their words)** (PO): "The chief aim of the founders
  and early friends of the College was to furnish the Church, and more especially their own
  branch of it, with a pious and learned ministry." [cnj-q001] The Synod of New York's own 1753
  appeal to the Church of Scotland states, "it is from the College of New Jersey only that we can
  expect a remedy" for the shortage of ministers, adding that "were the poor Indian savages
  sensible of their own case they would join in the cry, and beg for more missionaries to be sent
  to propagate the religion of Jesus among them." [cnj-q021, cnj-q022] The 1764 Account closes by
  ascribing to "the singular favor of Heaven on the means of instruction here used" that "many
  youth who have come to Nassau Hall for education, without any just sense of the obligations
  either of natural or revealed religion, have been here effectually reformed, become men of
  solid and rational piety." [cnj-q037]
- **M2 Distinctive stress (doctrine, piety, preaching, languages, evangelism, faith principle,
  self-support, other)** (PO): Piety and learning together, in that stated order of priority
  (Maclean's narrative observation, not itself a primary quotation, that Belcher and the
  Trustees "always mention religion first" — not separately quoted this session). Governor
  Belcher, replying to the Trustees, pledged to take the College "under my countenance and
  protection, as a seminary of true religion and good literature." [cnj-q023] "Happily for the
  College of New Jersey, it is not and never has been a State or a Church college," though
  closely tied in practice to the Presbyterian Synod of New York. [cnj-q043] The Charter itself
  bars any religious test for students (see F8). [cnj-q009, cnj-q010]
- **M3 What the founders explicitly rejected or warned against** (PO): A later (19th-century)
  College President, Ashbel Green, restated what he took to be the founders' design: that "the
  design of its foundation would be perverted if religion should ever be cultivated in it to the
  neglect of science, or science to the neglect of religion," rejecting equally a College that
  became "a religious house like a Monastery, or a Theological Seminary" and one in which
  "science should be taught... without connecting with it... the principles and practice of
  genuine piety." [cnj-q024] The 1764 Account states of student instruction generally that "care
  is taken to cherish a spirit of liberty and free enquiry; and not only to permit, but even
  encourage their right of private judgment, without presuming to dictate with an air of
  infallibility, or demanding an implicit assent to the decisions of the preceptor." [cnj-q033]
- **M4 Motto or watchword, with earliest source** (PS): NOT FOUND (searched: maclean1877-v1).
- **M5 Later leaders' restatements of the emphasis (with dates)** (PS): President Ashbel Green
  (President 1812-1822), in his own "Historical Sketch of the Origin and Design of the College,"
  quoted at length by Maclean, restates the founders' design as the union of religion and
  learning; date of Green's Sketch itself not given in the passage quoted. [cnj-q024]

## S — Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)**
  (PO): The College itself did not license or ordain; the Synod of New York's own petition to the
  Church of Scotland (1753) frames the College as the sole "remedy" for the Presbyterian
  churches' shortage of ministers across six colonies, implying that graduates proceeded from the
  College into ministry via the Synod and its Presbyteries, not via any College-issued license.
  [cnj-q021, cnj-q022] NOT FOUND (searched: maclean1877-v1): a distinct College sending or
  commissioning ceremony or document, comparable to a mission board's instructions.
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): The Synod of New York's 1753 appeal names the
  Presbyterian churches of "New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Maryland, Virginia, and
  Carolina" as dependent on the College for ministers, and further envisions missionary sending
  to "the poor Indian savages." [cnj-q021, cnj-q022] One graduate, Samuel Kirkland (class of
  1765), became "a missionary to the Seneca tribe of Indians." [cnj-q040]
- **S3 Numbers sent, by period, with source** (PS): "Of one hundred and thirty graduates of the
  College who were students during the presidency of Dr. Finley [1761-1766], fifty-nine became

  Maclean's own narrative states five of six first-class graduates and five of seven
