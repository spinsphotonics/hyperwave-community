# Dossier — University of Wittenberg (`wittenberg`)

Note on sourcing: this session fetched and read five documents in full. Two are genuine primary
texts in a standard public-domain English translation: Luther's 1524 open letter *To the
Councilmen of All Cities in Germany That They Establish and Maintain Christian Schools*
(document_id `luther1524-councilmen`) and his 1530 *A Sermon on Keeping Children in School*
(document_id `luther1530-sermon`), both from *Works of Martin Luther*, vol. IV (Philadelphia,
1931). Neither Melanchthon's 1518 inaugural oration *De corrigendis adolescentiae studiis* nor
his 1528/1538 *Unterricht der Visitatoren* (Instructions for the Visitors, which contains the
school order) was located this session as a full public-domain English text or a digitized Latin/
German original (see sources.csv, rows `melanchthon1518-oration-NOT-FETCHED` and
`melanchthon1528-visitatoren-NOT-FETCHED`); this is the chapter's largest gap. Two secondary
English-language books were also read in full: F. V. N. Painter, *Luther on Education* (1889),
whose historical introduction quotes a short excerpt, in translation, of the school-order portion
of the 1528/1538 *Unterricht* (referred to in the secondary literature as the "Saxony School
Plan") — this excerpt is cited PRIMARY-QUOTING (via Painter); and Rev. Joseph Stump, *Philip
Melanchthon* (1897), a biography that quotes several primary letters verbatim (cited
PRIMARY-QUOTING via Stump) and otherwise supplies secondary narrative (cited SECONDARY). A short
modern magazine article (Kreitzer 2021) was fetched for orientation only and is cited SECONDARY
where used; it contains no primary quotations.

## F — Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): The University of Wittenberg (Latin Academia Vitebergensis), nicknamed "Leucorea" (Greek for "White Mountain," a translation of Witten Berg) in the humanist style of the day; today Martin Luther University of Halle-Wittenberg. [SECONDARY: kreitzer2021-backwoods-school; SECONDARY: stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon, wittenberg-q021]
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): The university was founded in 1502 by Frederick the Wise, Elector of Saxony. [SECONDARY: stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon, wittenberg-q021] Melanchthon arrived at Wittenberg on 25 August 1518 to take up the chair of Greek, and delivered his inaugural lecture, titled (in this source's English rendering) "The Improvement of Studies for the Youth," four days later. [SECONDARY: stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon, wittenberg-q014] (Note: the roster and standard scholarship elsewhere give the oration's date as 28 or 29 August 1518; Stump's own narrative, read literally -- arrival 25 August plus "four days after" -- points to about 29 August. See discrepancies.md.) A Wittenberg statutes/Fundationsurkunde of 1536, associated in secondary literature with completing the arts-faculty reform, was not fetched or read this session; NOT FOUND (searched: no document located).
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PO/PS): Frederick the Wise, Elector of Saxony, founded the university (1502). [SECONDARY: wittenberg-q021] Martin Luther held the chair of Bible from the 1513-14 academic year and began pressing for curricular reform. Philipp Melanchthon (1497-1560) was recruited on Reuchlin's recommendation ("I know of no one among the Germans who excels him, except Erasmus of Rotterdam" is reported by Stump but not itself set off in quotation marks as a full sentence transcribed verbatim in the fetched text at the point checked, so it is not separately quote-recorded here) and took up the chair of Greek in 1518 at age twenty-one; Reuchlin's letter of call to Melanchthon survives and is quoted by Stump. [PRIMARY (via Stump): wittenberg-q013] Melanchthon served as rector in 1523 and 1524 and used that office to advance curriculum reform. [SECONDARY: kreitzer2021-backwoods-school]
- **F4 Place(s)** (PO): Wittenberg, Electoral Saxony. NOT FOUND (searched: luther1524-councilmen, luther1530-sermon, painter1889-lutheroneducation, stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon) for any additional site detail (e.g., specific buildings) beyond the town itself.
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): No single "founding charter" of the reformed Wittenberg curriculum was fetched and read this session. The two documents actually read and abridgeable are Luther's 1524 open letter *To the Councilmen of All Cities in Germany* (document_id `luther1524-councilmen`) and his 1530 *A Sermon on Keeping Children in School* (document_id `luther1530-sermon`) -- both addressed to German cities and pastors generally, not statutes of Wittenberg itself, but standard primary statements of the Wittenberg circle's educational program, explicitly named in the roster. Melanchthon's 1518 inaugural oration and his 1528/1538 *Unterricht der Visitatoren* (which Stump confirms "added a chapter on the improvements to be made in the schools" [PRIMARY (via Stump): wittenberg-q019]) are the documents the roster names as Wittenberg's own founding texts, but neither was located and read in full this session; only a short excerpt of the school-order portion of the *Unterricht*, at second hand via Painter's 1889 translation, was obtained (see C2/C3 below). This is the chapter's central BLOCKED item; see discrepancies.md.
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): Luther's 1524 letter states, as its opening complaint, that schools throughout Germany are being allowed to "go to wrack and ruin" and universities to grow weak. [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q001] He argues that a city's "best and highest welfare, safety and strength consist in its having many able, learned, wise, honorable and well-bred citizens," not in treasure, walls, or armaments. [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q002] Considered purely as a matter of temporal government (setting the question of the soul aside), he asks whether the "temporal government" does not "need good schools and educated persons more sorely even than the spiritual realm." [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q004]
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PO): Luther states that with the decline of the old ecclesiastical culture (monasteries, cathedral schools) "how schools are everywhere allowed to go to wrack and ruin," because parents no longer see the point of an education aimed at the priesthood, monastic, or conventual life, and ask instead "why should we send them to school, if they are not to become priests, monks and nuns?" (paraphrased from the passage surrounding wittenberg-q001; the direct quotation recorded is the "wrack and ruin" clause itself). In the 1530 Sermon, Luther names the devil's chief current stratagem as deceiving "the common people so that they are not willing to keep their children in school." [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q005]
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): No matriculation oath or confession-subscription clause for Wittenberg itself was located in the material read this session. The one doctrinal-basis-adjacent primary-quoting passage found is from Melanchthon's 1528 *Unterricht der Visitatoren* (via Painter and, separately, via Stump): the school-order portion instructs that "the school-master shall impress upon the children those truths that are necessary to right living, as the fear of God, faith, and good works," and explicitly "shall not speak of polemical matters." [PRIMARY (via Painter): wittenberg-q012] The same work's doctrinal chapter (on the clergy, not narrowly the schools) defines Christian liberty as "freedom from the power of the devil and the wrath of God; freedom from the ceremonial law of Moses; freedom from absolute obedience to human regulations in the Church." [PRIMARY (via Stump): wittenberg-q018]
- **F9 Antecedents the founders cite or imitate (earlier schools, documents, persons)** (PS): NOT FOUND as a passage in which Luther or Melanchthon themselves name an earlier school as their model, in the material read this session. Painter's narrative states only that the "Saxony School Plan" was "originally prepared by Melancthon and revised by Luther in 1538" [PRIMARY-adjacent (via Painter): wittenberg-q009] -- i.e., it documents Luther's and Melanchthon's own relationship to that later document, not an antecedent they themselves cited.

## C — Curriculum
- **C1 Length of course** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: same four documents) for a specific number of years at the university level. Stump records only that Melanchthon delivered lectures on Homer and on the Epistle to Titus in his first announced course, and separately took up teaching Hebrew. [SECONDARY: stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon, wittenberg-q017]
- **C2 Subjects, in the order taught** (PO): At the level of the feeder Latin schools, the school-order excerpt instructs that "the teachers shall see to it that the children are taught only Latin, not German or Hebrew as some have hitherto done, who have burdened their pupils with too many studies, which are not only useless but hurtful," and that teachers "shall not burden the children with many books, but in every way avoid a distracting multiplicity of studies." [PRIMARY (via Painter): wittenberg-q010] At the university, Melanchthon's own announced subjects were the poems of Homer and Paul's Epistle to Titus (Greek), with Hebrew added soon after. [SECONDARY: stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon, wittenberg-q017] Kreitzer's orientation article states that as rector (1523-24) Melanchthon "introduced Greek and Hebrew as independent subjects" and added rhetoric, history, and poetics to the arts course. [SECONDARY: kreitzer2021-backwoods-school]
- **C3 Structure (classes, years, terms)** (PO): The school-order excerpt states as its third governing principle: "It is necessary that the children be divided into grades." [PRIMARY (via Painter): wittenberg-q011] No further detail of how many grades, or the university's own faculty structure (arts before theology, etc.), was found quoted in the material read this session; Kreitzer's secondary account states the arts course (trivium: grammar, logic, rhetoric) preceded the higher faculties (theology among them) and that Melanchthon's Loci Communes (1521) and, after 1530, the Augsburg Confession became required texts. [SECONDARY: kreitzer2021-backwoods-school]
- **C4 Languages taught, and level required** (PO): Latin (the ordinary language of instruction; the school-order excerpt explicitly restricts the lower schools to Latin, excluding German or Hebrew there [PRIMARY (via Painter): wittenberg-q010]); Greek and Hebrew at the university level. Luther's 1524 letter argues at length for the necessity of the biblical languages, calling them "the sheath in which this sword of the Spirit is contained ... the casket in which we carry this jewel ... the vessel in which we hold this wine ... the larder in which this food is stored." [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q003]
- **C5 Set texts and authors** (PO): Homer and Paul's Epistle to Titus, in Melanchthon's first announced lecture course; he had a special edition of Titus printed because copies of Erasmus's Greek New Testament were scarce at Wittenberg (this printing detail is Stump's narrative, not separately quote-recorded). [SECONDARY: stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon, wittenberg-q017] After 1521, Melanchthon's own Loci Communes; after 1530, the Augsburg Confession. [SECONDARY: kreitzer2021-backwoods-school]
- **C6 Preaching or practical instruction (form, frequency)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: same four documents).
- **C7 Examinations and their form** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: same four documents).
- **C8 Degree, certificate, license, or ordination at completion** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: same four documents). Kreitzer's secondary account states that most students sought only basic arts degrees, which qualified them to become tutors or teachers in Latin schools, and, after the Reformation, to prepare for pastorates. [SECONDARY: kreitzer2021-backwoods-school]
- **C9 Weekly or daily timetable (table if given)** (PO): No student timetable was found. Melanchthon's own personal working day is described by Stump (his household routine, not a university-wide rule): he rose "regularly at two o'clock in the morning," family worship was held at seven followed by breakfast, and he lectured at the university "from nine till eleven." [SECONDARY: stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon, wittenberg-q020]

- **L7 Number of students (with years)** (PS): "In the year 1517 there had been no more than two hundred students enrolled" at Wittenberg; after Melanchthon's arrival, numbers grew steadily until "the number of his auditors constantly increased till they reached a thousand and even two thousand or more." [SECONDARY: stump1897-lifeofmelanchthon, wittenberg-q015, wittenberg-q016] Kreitzer states Wittenberg became the largest university in Germany in 1520 and that Luther's lectures might draw up to 400 students, Melanchthon's 400 to 600. Stump notes the university itself was twice disrupted -- removed to Jena in 1527 on account of plague in Wittenberg, and later suppressed and restored around 1547-48 during the Smalcald War -- but no student casualty figures were found.

## M — Emphasis
- **M1 What the founders said mattered most (their words)** (PO): "A city's best and highest welfare, safety and strength consist in its having many able, learned, wise, honorable and well-bred citizens." [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q002] On languages specifically: "The languages are the sheath in which this sword of the Spirit is contained; they are the casket in which we carry this jewel." [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q003]
- **M2 Distinctive stress (doctrine, piety, preaching, languages, evangelism, faith principle, self-support, other)** (PO): The biblical languages (Hebrew and Greek), as the necessary means of access to Scripture itself rather than to translations or scholastic commentary, and the connection Luther draws between a well-schooled citizenry and both the spiritual and the "temporal" welfare of a city. [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q003, wittenberg-q002, wittenberg-q004]
- **M3 What the founders explicitly rejected or warned against** (PO): Neglect of children's schooling under the excuse that, since not every boy will become a priest, monk, or nun, education is pointless; Luther calls this neglect one of the devil's own "wiles." [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q005] He contrasts German neglect unfavorably with the Ottoman practice (as he understood and reported it) of systematically training selected children for state service: "The Turk ... takes every third child in his whole empire and trains it for what he will." [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q008]
- **M4 Motto or watchword, with earliest source** (PS): NOT FOUND (searched: same four documents). Melanchthon is called "Preceptor Germaniae" (Teacher of Germany) in Stump's narrative, describing a later reputation rather than a founding-era motto of the institution itself, and the passage is not set off in quotation marks as a primary title conferred by any dated source, so it is not separately quote-recorded or used as a dossier fact here.
- **M5 Later leaders' restatements of the emphasis (with dates)** (PS): NOT FOUND (searched: same four documents).

## S — Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)** (PO): Wittenberg did not "send" graduates through a missionary-society mechanism in the sense later chapters of this book describe. Luther argues instead that civil government itself has the duty to compel school attendance because the community depends on a supply of "preachers, jurists, pastors, writers, physicians, schoolmasters, and the like": "I hold that it is the duty of the government to compel its subjects to keep their children in school." [PRIMARY: wittenberg-q007] Graduates went out chiefly as pastors, teachers in town Latin schools, and (per Kreitzer's secondary account) as university professors and reform leaders in Lutheran, Calvinist, and Swiss churches, but no primary sending register or licensing clause was located and read this session. [SECONDARY: kreitzer2021-backwoods-school]
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): UNVERIFIED this session beyond "all Germany" and, per Kreitzer's secondary orientation, "around Germany" and internationally as students carried Wittenberg's teaching home. [SECONDARY: kreitzer2021-backwoods-school]
