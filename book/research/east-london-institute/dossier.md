# Dossier -- East London Institute for Home and Foreign Missions (`east-london-institute`)

## F -- Founding
- **F1 Name(s) of the institution, with dates of each name** (PS): The institution's own books call it "the East London Institute for Home and Foreign Missions" throughout (title of `wideworldourwork00guin`, 1886, and named in the founding sentence, eli-q001). Its two teaching buildings are separately named "Harley College" (men, Bow, from 1879) and "Doric Lodge" (women, from 1885); an 1894 pamphlet's table of contents calls the whole enterprise "the First English Interdenominational Training Home for Missionary Volunteers" [eli-q041]. It later developed into "the Regions Beyond Missionary Union" under the founders' son [SECONDARY: drharryguinnessl0000unse, eli-q044]. A missionary magazine, "Regions Beyond," began in 1887 under Mrs. Guinness's editorship [SECONDARY: drharryguinnessl0000unse, eli-q048].
- **F2 Founding date(s): decision, opening, charter/incorporation** (PS): Sources disagree between 1872 and 1873; both are given without resolution -- see `discrepancies.md` §1. Two Institute publications state 1872: "led to the foundation, in 1872, of our East London Institute" [eli-q001]; "opened their Missionary College there early in 1872" [eli-q035]. Two other sources state 1873: "the estab[l]ishment in 1873 of a Training Institute in East London" [eli-q049]; "since the Inauguration of the Work in 1873" [eli-q024]; and [SECONDARY: drharryguinnessl0000unse] "In the same year [1873] his parents established the East London Institute" [eli-q044]. `notuntousarecord00guinuoft` (p.9-10, narrative context of eli-q049) additionally states the Institute moved to Harley House in 1874, Cliff College (Derbyshire) was added the following year, and Harley College itself (the purpose-built men's building) was erected in 1879.
- **F3 Founders and first officers (names, roles)** (PS): "Dr. and Mrs. H. Grattan Guinness opened their Missionary College" [eli-q035] -- i.e., Henry Grattan Guinness (1835-1910) and his wife Fanny Emma Guinness (1831-1898), jointly. Fanny Guinness is separately named as the author of the Institute's own 1886 account and, per the secondary biography, as editor of the *Regions Beyond* magazine from 1887 [eli-q048]. The Rev. Anson J. Towell is named as "our headmaster" in an 1880s tutor's report [eli-q008].
- **F4 Place(s)** (PS): Bow, East London (Harley House, later Harley College); a "Country Branch," Cliff College / Hulme Cliff, near Curbar/Bakewell, Derbyshire (from the 1874-75 removal noted under F2); Doric Lodge, opposite Harley House on the Bow Road, added 1884-85 for women students [eli-q015, eli-q016].
- **F5 Principal founding document: title, date, language, document_id** (PO): *The Wide World and Our Work in It: or, the Story of the East London Institute for Home and Foreign Missions*, by Mrs. H. Grattan Guinness, London: Hodder and Stoughton, 1886, English, document_id `wideworldourwork00guin`, Chapter II ("Our Institute, and Why We Founded It"). No separately titled charter, constitution, or numbered "Principles and Practice" document (of the kind CIM has) was located for this Institute; this founder-authored institutional history is used as the principal founding document, per the roster's own [VERIFY] flag on a separate "Prospectus" (see `sources.csv` row `eli-prospectus-not-found`).
- **F6 Stated purpose, in the founders' words** (PO): The Institute existed "through which earnest young Christians of the self-supporting class, spiritually fitted for missionary work, but disqualified by lack of education and practical training, might be prepared Biblically, Intellectually, and Practically, and sent abroad when trained" [eli-q033], founded because the existing missionary societies' preference for university-trained "officers" left willing but poorer men unused: "Did any army consisting of officers only ever march to victory?" [eli-q003]. It aimed to be "a link that should unite" an "abundant but unutilized supply" of willing workers to "a terrible lack of workers" on the mission field (paraphrase of the surrounding Chapter II narrative around eli-q003; not separately quote-recorded beyond what is already cited).
- **F7 Need or crisis the founders said they were answering** (PO): Contact with "large numbers of Christian young men...filled with love and zeal, longing to give their lives to the service of Christ, but for whose service in the gospel there seemed to be little opening" [eli-q002], combined with a conviction that existing societies "were totally inadequate" to the task (paraphrase, same passage). A parallel 1894 statement gives the same crisis in numerical terms: "over eight hundred millions have never heard of Christ" [eli-q032], and states the founders' operating principle that "before men are sent forth to do missionary work amongst the heathen, their ability for it should be practically tested, and, when found to exist, be developed" [eli-q034].
- **F8 Doctrinal basis or confession, and who must subscribe** (PO): No single numbered doctrinal statement (of the kind CIM's Article 7 gives) was located. The Institute is repeatedly described as deliberately undenominational: "the proposed Institute could evidently have NO DENOMINATIONAL CONNECTIONS OR SUPPORT. It would have to depend on God and on those of His people whose hearts embrace all who love our Lord Jesus Christ in sincerity" [eli-q004]; and, later, "Harley College is as interdenominational as it is international" [eli-q028], training "Baptists and Episcopalians, Congregationalists, Wesleyans and Presbyterians" side by side (context of eli-q028, `notuntousarecord00guinuoft` p.39; not separately quote-recorded). It had, by design, no committee, advisory council, or endowment: "They had no committee behind them, no advisory council, and no sort of endowment" [eli-q036].
- **F9 Antecedents the founders cite or imitate (earlier schools, documents, persons)** (PO): The secondary biography states the Institute's origin traced to three sources, the second being the Guinnesses' meeting with Hudson Taylor, "when he had just returned from Inland China overwhelmed with the sense of its spiritual destitution" [SECONDARY: drharryguinnessl0000unse, eli-q045]. An Institute-published 1894 pamphlet makes the same connection from the other direction, describing how "in the early days of the China Inland Mission, twenty-five years ago, among the volunteers who offered to Mr. Hudson Taylor were two whom he advised to stay at home" [eli-q042] -- one of whom, Thomas J. Barnardo, "has probably done more for the Kingdom of Christ here than he would have done had his early hope of work in China been realized" [eli-q043]. `notuntousarecord00guinuoft` separately records that H. Grattan Guinness had, twenty-one years before 1887, run a Bible class in Dublin ("Paley's [Horae] Paulines," rendered by the OCR as "HOYCB Paulines") [eli-q022] whose students included "two young men destined to occupy important spheres of service -- Thomas J. Barnardo and John McCarthy, subsequently of the China Inland Mission" [eli-q023], making this Dublin class a direct forerunner in the founders' own later telling.

## A -- Admission
- **A1 Minimum and maximum age** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: wideworldourwork00guin, notuntousarecord00guinuoft, ourhelpersatwork00guin). No source read this session states a minimum or maximum admission age.
- **A2 Prior education required** (PO): No formal prior education is stated as required; the founders explicitly designed the Institute for men "disqualified by lack of education and practical training" [eli-q033], ranking "acquired ability and educational attainments" last among what they sought in an applicant, after "sincere piety" and "natural ability" [eli-q006]. A minimum fee of £20 was charged, but "we have never yet refused a downright good man, simply because he could not afford to pay" [eli-q026].
- **A3 Testimony of conversion, character, or church membership required** (PO): "We seek sincere piety and true consecration of spirit, in the first place...No training can give a man either grace or strength of character; and we consequently look for these indispensable requisites before accepting an applicant" [eli-q006, eli-q007]. Unsuitable applicants were "simply declined" [eli-q005]; those accepted were "received on probation," with "full admission to the privileges of our training house...only accorded after a month or two of actual experience of the individual" [context of eli-q005/eli-q006, `wideworldourwork00guin` p.59]. By 1893, "all candidates must have given proof of evangelistic enthusiasm at home" before acceptance [eli-q027].
- **A4 Entrance examination (subjects)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: wideworldourwork00guin, notuntousarecord00guinuoft) as a formal subject-based entrance examination. Applications were instead treated as individual "cases": by 1894, "the first handful of applicants for missionary training has increased to 3,700 'cases,' each of which has been carefully considered, sifted, examined, and decided upon" [eli-q040].
- **A5 Women: admitted? in what capacity?** (PO): Yes, from 1884-85, in a separate branch: "the most recent addition to the Institute has been A FEMALE BRANCH for training young women for the mission field," begun because "every missionary's wife ought herself to be a missionary, not only in heart, but in actual practical suitability" [eli-q016]; housed at Doric Lodge from early 1885, this branch "has since sent out many young sisters, some as wives and some single, to China, North Africa, the Congo, South America, and other lands" [eli-q015].
- **A6 Prior experience required (e.g., preaching, service)** (PO): By 1893, "before they are accepted, all candidates must have given proof of evangelistic enthusiasm at home. Missionary sentiment may grow out of missionary study, but missionary passion can come from nothing but actual work" [eli-q027]. No source read this session states a specific minimum period of prior service, unlike Spurgeon's Pastors' College (per the roster's known-facts sheet).

## C -- Curriculum
- **C1 Length of course** (PO): Two figures, from different years, are given rather than merged -- see `discrepancies.md` §4. By 1893: "our present course extends over a period of four years" [eli-q025]. Medical students required "a much longer time" (paraphrase, `wideworldourwork00guin` p.29-30, in the founding-era planning discussion around eli-q001-eli-q004; not separately quote-recorded).
- **C2 Subjects, in the order taught** (PO): An 1880s tutor's report names, in order of discussion, Bible-classes, Theological Classes, historical studies, and classes in Greek and Hebrew [eli-q009, eli-q010, eli-q011]. By 1893 the curriculum was organized into three named departments -- "THE BIBLICAL DEPARTMENT," "THE GENERAL EDUCATION DEPARTMENT," and "THE MISSIONARY DEPARTMENT" -- each with a numbered list of subjects (`notuntousarecord00guinuoft` p.41-42, headed by eli-q030): the Biblical Department covering Devotional Study, Biblical Introduction, the Prophets, Messianic Prophecy, the Gospels, the Life and Missionary Work of St. Paul, and the Epistles; the General Education Department covering English Language and Literature, Rhetoric, Logic, Psychology, Ethics, Languages (Greek, and for certain students Hebrew, French, Spanish), and Apologetics, Theology, and Homiletics; the Missionary Department covering the World's Religions, the Comparison of Religions, and the History, Methods, and Results of Missions; plus a fourth, "THE PRACTICAL MISSIONARY TRAINING," covering health of soul and body, conduct, dispensary work, hospital medical training, weekly preaching, and "Prayer, Faith, and Mutual Forbearance" (same passage, not separately quote-recorded).
- **C3 Structure (classes, years, terms)** (PO): Organized by department (see C2) rather than by numbered year-classes as at Geneva or Andover; no term/semester structure is stated in any source read this session.
- **C4 Languages taught, and level required** (PO): "In the classes for Greek and Hebrew the men have made some fair progress" [eli-q010]. By 1893: "The Bible is our classic -- in English, in Greek, and for some students, in Hebrew" [eli-q031], with French and Spanish also taught to "certain students" (context of eli-q030, `notuntousarecord00guinuoft` p.42). No level or examination standard is stated.
- **C5 Set texts and authors** (PO): Only the Bible itself is named as a set text ("The Bible is our classic," eli-q031); no other author or textbook is named in any source read this session, beyond the founders' own Dublin-class use of Paley (see F9, eli-q022) which predates and is not stated to be part of the Institute's own curriculum.
- **C6 Preaching or practical instruction (form, frequency)** (PO): Extensive: open-air preaching in East London (Mile End Waste, Whitechapel, Bethnal Green, Limehouse, Shadwell), lodging-house and hospital visiting, work among sailors and orientals at the Asiatic Home, and (by 1893) "constant week-end preaching" as part of the fourth, practical department (context of eli-q030). "The general discipline and training of the men, the house-work and the practice of useful arts, has proved highly beneficial" -- including gardening, shoemaking, forge-work, and swimming [eli-q011].
- **C7 Examinations and their form** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: wideworldourwork00guin, notuntousarecord00guinuoft) as a described formal examination procedure or schedule, beyond the general statement that the men were "examined" via the probation period (A3) and that applicant "cases" were "examined" before admission (A4, eli-q040) -- these describe screening, not academic testing during the course.
- **C8 Degree, certificate, license, or ordination at completion** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: wideworldourwork00guin, notuntousarecord00guinuoft). No source read this session describes a degree, diploma, or ordination conferred on completion; students were instead placed directly with one of "forty missionary organizations" that "number our men amongst their workers" [eli-q029].
- **C9 Weekly or daily timetable (table if given)** (PO): A daily order at Cliff College is given in narrative form (see L1) rather than as a table; no source read this session gives a tabulated weekly timetable.

## L -- Common life
- **L1 Daily order: rising, worship, meals, study, bed** (PO): At Cliff College: breakfast "as usual, at 7.30" [eli-q012]; "at 8 o'clock they gathered with the whole household for reading," with hymn-singing, Scripture exposition, and prayer for the Institute and its outgoing missionaries (context of eli-q012); "at 9 o'clock the gong sounded for studies" [eli-q013]; the afternoon given to farm and manual work (gardening, forge, carpentry, errands); "at 6 o'clock in the evening, after tea, all will again be gathered in the classroom for quiet study, keeping steadily to their books until 9 o'clock," "when the household assembles for evening prayers" [eli-q014]. (Full context of the daily order is in `wideworldourwork00guin` p.73-74, around eli-q012/eli-q013/eli-q014.)
- **L2 Residence: where students lived; with whom** (PS): Men resided at Harley House/Harley College, Bow; women at Doric Lodge, opposite, "to accommodate twenty students, together with the offices of the Institution and the family of the lady superintendent" (context, `wideworldourwork00guin` p.118, around eli-q016; not separately quote-recorded). Students also spent time at Cliff College, Derbyshire ("Our Galilee," their "refuge" from London, context around eli-q013).
- **L3 Worship: form and frequency** (PO): Morning household reading/exposition and prayer, and evening prayers, as in L1 [eli-q014 and context]; by 1893, "the morning hour of meditation, the daily exposition at College prayers" (`notuntousarecord00guinuoft` p.41, narrative context near eli-q025/eli-q027; not separately quote-recorded).
- **L4 Discipline: rules and penalties** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: wideworldourwork00guin, notuntousarecord00guinuoft) as a stated code of penalties. General character-testing during probation (A3) served this function; "no training can give a man either grace or strength of character" [eli-q007] is the closest statement of principle located.
- **L5 Dress, conduct, recreation rules** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: wideworldourwork00guin, notuntousarecord00guinuoft) as a stated dress or conduct code (contrast CIM's Chinese-dress requirement, cited in the roster's known-facts sheet for that institution). Manual work "on his working jacket" is mentioned narratively at Cliff (`wideworldourwork00guin` p.73-74, context of eli-q013) but not as a rule.
- **L6 Fees, board, and how students were supported** (PO): "In spite of the fact that our minimum fee is £20, we have never yet refused a downright good man, simply because he could not afford to pay" [eli-q026]. The Institute itself had, by design, "no committee behind them, no advisory council, and no sort of endowment" [eli-q036], depending instead on "those of His people whose hearts embrace all who love our Lord Jesus Christ" [eli-q004].
- **L7 Number of students (with years)** (PS): See `discrepancies.md` §2 for the several distinct counts at different dates: ~400-500 former students "now scattered" (1886) [eli-q017]; 800 labourers in the field and 120 in training (1894) [eli-q039]; 1,316 total trained since 1873 (1893) [eli-q024]; c.1,500 total sent, undated (SECONDARY, [1932]) [eli-q044].
- **L8 Health, deaths, or hardship recorded among students** (PS): Mr. Bowers, sent to Brazil, died of yellow fever shortly after arrival: "He was the last of the seventy to leave the Institute, and the first to reach his heavenly home" [eli-q019]. Fourteen brethren and sisters died in the Institute's Congo (Livingstone Inland Mission) work; see S5.

## M -- Emphasis
- **M1 What the founders said mattered most (their words)** (PO): That practical fitness, not scholarly credentials, qualified a man for mission work: "Did any army consisting of officers only ever march to victory?" [eli-q003]; and that such fitness "should be practically tested, and, when found to exist, be developed by missionary work at home" before a man was sent abroad [eli-q034].
- **M2 Distinctive stress (doctrine, piety, preaching, languages, evangelism, faith principle, self-support, other)** (PO): Deliberate interdenominationalism combined with practical, home-based evangelistic testing: "Harley College is as interdenominational as it is international" [eli-q028]; no denominational "connections or support" [eli-q004]; piety and character sought "in the first place," "acquired ability and educational attainments last" [eli-q006].
- **M3 What the founders explicitly rejected or warned against** (PO): The assumption "that highly cultured and university trained men alone should be sent out to the foreign field" as the only workers needed (paraphrase, `wideworldourwork00guin` p.24-25, the "army of officers" passage around eli-q003; not separately quote-recorded beyond eli-q003 itself). They also rejected sending untested volunteers: "to encourage and send out untried and untrained men to undertake this task were clearly folly" (same passage, context of eli-q003-eli-q004; not separately quote-recorded).
- **M4 Motto or watchword, with earliest source** (PS): "The house motto of Regions Beyond" is named directly in the secondary biography [SECONDARY: drharryguinnessl0000unse, eli-q046], drawn from the name later given to the missionary magazine (1887) and the wider missionary union; the phrase echoes 2 Corinthians 10:16 ("to preach the gospel in the regions beyond you"), though no source read this session quotes that verse in direct connection with the motto's adoption, so that connection is not asserted as sourced.
- **M5 Later leaders' restatements of the emphasis (with dates)** (PS): By 1893, under the founders' son as an increasingly active co-director, the same practical/evangelistic emphasis is restated in a more organized, departmental form (see C2), while retaining the founding stress on "the passion of devotion to CHRIST, of loyalty to His Gospel, and of love to men" (`notuntousarecord00guinuoft` p.42, narrative context near eli-q030/eli-q031; not separately quote-recorded).

## S -- Sending
- **S1 Sending mechanism (licensing body, mission board, church commendation, self-sending)** (PO): The Institute trained men and women and placed them with existing societies rather than itself commissioning or licensing them: "after fitting them for their noble calling, it gives them back as missionaries to their own societies. No less than forty missionary organizations now number our men amongst their workers" [eli-q029].
- **S2 Destinations (regions, countries)** (PS): Very broad. Named destinations across the sources read include China (repeatedly -- see R1, R2), Brazil (eli-q019/eli-q020), Newfoundland, Nova Scotia, Bombay, and Africa (context, `wideworldourwork00guin` p.59, around eli-q005-eli-q007); the Congo (via the Livingstone Inland Mission, S5); North Africa/Algiers, South America, and elsewhere for the women's branch (eli-q015). "Thirty or forty lands have been represented by the students" as their countries of origin, separately from destinations [eli-q039 context, `ourhelpersatwork00guin` p.6].
- **S3 Numbers sent, by period, with source** (PS): See L7 and `discrepancies.md` §2 for the full set of distinct figures at different dates (1886: ~400-500 currently serving [eli-q017]; 1893: 1,316 total trained [eli-q024]; 1894: 800 labourers plus 120 in training [eli-q039], and separately "over 100" to China plus "600 or 700" elsewhere [eli-q038]; undated, secondary, c.1,500 total [eli-q044]).
- **S4 Support on the field (salary, faith principle, self-support)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: wideworldourwork00guin, notuntousarecord00guinuoft) as a stated field-support policy comparable to CIM's "no guaranteed salary" article. The Institute is described as training men "of the self-supporting class" before sending [eli-q033], but no source read this session states what financial arrangement, if any, applied once a missionary reached the field, beyond the general "faith" character of the Institute's own home finances (L6).
- **S5 Deaths, martyrdoms, or casualties recorded, with source** (PS): The Institute's Congo mission (the Livingstone Inland Mission, founded from the Institute in 1878) is recorded as having lost fourteen workers: "Fourteen brethren and sisters laid down their lives in the prosecution of this enterprise (three of whom were coloured men), and a still larger number were invalided home. Fifty missionaries in all were sent out" [eli-q018]. Separately, Mr. Bowers, sent to Brazil, died of yellow fever soon after arrival, "the last of the seventy to leave the Institute, and the first to reach his heavenly home" [eli-q019].
- **S6 Instructions given to those sent (title, date, document_id)** (PO): NOT FOUND (searched: wideworldourwork00guin, notuntousarecord00guinuoft). No source read this session names or quotes a separate written instructions document given to departing missionaries, comparable to the ABCFM's 1812 *Instructions* to Judson's party (cited elsewhere in the roster).

## R -- Fruit
- **R1** Frederick William Baller: former Institute student who, "in 1880," "in a long journey across the centre of the empire to Kwei-chau in the south-west, escorted a party of missionary sisters to their distant destination," itinerating extensively for the China Inland Mission [eli-q020].
- **R2** George Parker: former Institute student and China Inland Mission missionary in Kan-suh (Gansu), who wrote of having "visited most of the cities in northern Kan-suh, which have thus had the gospel brought to them for the first time," travelling "3,200 miles" in "208 days" and selling "10,000 portions of Scripture" (context of eli-q021, `wideworldourwork00guin` p.135-136; the introductory sentence itself is eli-q021).
- **R3** Thomas John Barnardo (1845-1905): attended H. Grattan Guinness's Dublin Bible class in the mid-1860s, one of "two young men destined to occupy important spheres of service" alongside John McCarthy [eli-q023]; the same 1894 pamphlet records that, having originally hoped for China, "Dr. Barnardo has probably done more for the Kingdom of Christ here [in London] than he would have done had his early hope of work in China been realized," through "nearly 26,000 orphans and waif children" passed through his hands [eli-q043].
- **R4** "Mr. Timothy Richard," a former student of Harley House found by Harry Guinness in 1891 "in charge" of the American Baptist Missionary Union's station at Banza Manteke, Congo [SECONDARY: drharryguinnessl0000unse, eli-q047]. See `discrepancies.md` §5: this is not stated by any source read this session to be the well-known China missionary of the same name.
- Bessie Nelson, of Sidon, Syria, a former student of the women's branch, is quoted at length on the practical preparation a missionary lady needs (`wideworldourwork00guin` p.119-120, narrative context near eli-q016; not separately quote-recorded as a one-sentence outcome, so not given its own numbered entry here).
